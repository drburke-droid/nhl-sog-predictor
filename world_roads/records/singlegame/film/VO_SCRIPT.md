# Narration script: The 10 Biggest Single-Day Outliers in Sports History

The film works without a voice; every point is on screen. Timecodes are positions in the final cut (9:49). Each card
is 36 seconds, so keep each one under about 75 words. Never say "proved", "greatest athlete" or "certain".

## Hook (0:00–0:30)

**0:01** Two thousand seven hundred and ninety-seven single-day bests: one game, one race, one jump at a time.

**0:07** Each one graded against the best day of everyone else in its era.

**0:10** Most land near zero. Only three reach five.

**0:17** Ten broke the curve.

**0:25** These are the ten biggest single-day outliers in sports history, counting down from ten.

## #10 Johannes Vetter, 2020 (0:29)

**0:31** Number ten. Johannes Vetter, 2020: ninety-seven seventy-six with the javelin.

**0:35** Each grey javelin is the longest throw by anyone else in a season, fifteen years either side. The dashed ring
is the curve; each ring beyond it is one step.

**0:48** The next best that year was nearly ten metres shorter. Only Železný's world record has gone further.

## #9 Léon Marchand, 2025 (1:04)

**1:06** Nine. Léon Marchand, 2025: the two hundred metres medley in one fifty-two sixty-nine.

**1:15** Imagine the fastest swimmer of every other season in the pool with him. When he touches the wall, the curve is
still four metres back.

**1:25** It broke Ryan Lochte's world record, which had stood since 2011. 2025 has only past seasons to compare with.

## #8 Sergey Bubka, 1993 (1:40)

**1:42** Eight. Sergey Bubka, indoors in Donetsk, 1993: six metres fifteen.

**1:55** Each grey bar is the highest anyone else cleared that season. Bubka's bar climbs four steps above the curve.

**2:05** It stood as the world record for twenty-one years.

## #7 Brian Oldfield, 1975 (2:15)

**2:17** Seven. Brian Oldfield, El Paso, 1975: a shot put of twenty-two eighty-six.

**2:30** It was thrown at a professional meet and never ratified. The official world record was more than a metre
shorter.

## #6 Yulimar Rojas, 2021 (2:51)

**2:53** Six. Yulimar Rojas, Tokyo 2021: fifteen sixty-seven in the triple jump.

**3:05** A world record by seventeen centimetres, beating a mark that had stood since 1995.

## #5 Katie Ledecky, 2016 (3:26)

**3:28** Five. Katie Ledecky, Rio 2016: the eight hundred metres freestyle in eight oh four seventy-nine.

**3:40** When she touched the wall, the curve was nineteen metres behind her. The silver medallist finished more than
eleven seconds later.

## #4 Kobe Bryant, 2005–06 (4:02)

**4:04** Four. Kobe Bryant, 22 January 2006: eighty-one points against Toronto, fifty-five of them in the second half.

**4:20** Only one player has scored more in an NBA game.

## #3 Jan Železný, 1996 (4:37)

**4:39** Three. Jan Železný, 1996: ninety-eight forty-eight. Thirty years on, still the world record.

## #2 Wilt Chamberlain, 1961–62 (5:12)

**5:14** Two. Wilt Chamberlain, Hershey, Pennsylvania, 2 March 1962: one hundred points.

**5:30** No one else that season scored more than sixty-three in a game. Even allowing for how much teams scored then,
it sits more than seven steps above its era.

## #1 Bob Beamon, 1968 (5:48)

**5:50** And number one, again: Bob Beamon, Mexico City, 1968. One jump, eight ninety. Fifty-five centimetres beyond
the world record, and nobody jumped further for twenty-three years.

## Why hockey and home runs aren't here (6:23)

**6:25** Darryl Sittler scored ten points in one game in 1976. It's still the NHL record.

**6:31** But around it, everyone else's best night was usually seven points, and six times it was eight. Ten is
about four steps: sixteenth, not top ten.

**6:45** Goals, home runs and hits come in ones. When most seasons' best night is the same small number, one more
can look enormous, or like nothing. So this list keeps the stats that come in big numbers.

## How the score works: grading on a curve (7:11)

**7:13** So how does the score work? Picture a class test. Most of the class scores around seventy, give or take
about five points.

**7:20** Call that usual give-or-take one step.

**7:26** A student who scores ninety-five is twenty-five points above the class average. That's five steps, so on
our scale it's a score of five.

**7:37** Put the same ninety-five in a class that averages ninety, and it's one step above: a score of one.

**7:46** Same number, different class. Every day in this film was graded against the class of its own era: the
best day by anyone else in each of the fifteen seasons before and after.

## Three rules that keep it fair (7:54)

**7:56** Three rules keep it fair. First, inflation. When every team scores more, each point is worth a little less,
so in team sports everyone else's games are converted into the athlete's own season before they're compared.

**8:07** Second, you're graded against your rivals, not yourself. Otherwise Wilt's hundred would be graded against
Wilt's seventy-eight.

**8:15** Third, one entry each, and only stats that come in big numbers.

## A season or a day? (8:34)

**8:35** The same method, two questions. Over a season, consistency stands out: Gretzky, DiMaggio, Ruth, Curry. Over
a single game, team sports are noisy, and only Wilt's hundred and Kobe's eighty-one clear the bar.

**9:08** So this isn't a ranking of the greatest athletes. It's how far one day stood from everyone else of its
time. Which would you choose: the season, or the day?
