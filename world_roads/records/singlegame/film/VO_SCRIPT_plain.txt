Two thousand seven hundred and ninety-seven single-day bests: one game, one race, one jump at a time.

Each one graded against the best day of everyone else in its era.

Most land near zero. Only three reach five.

Ten broke the curve.

These are the ten biggest single-day outliers in sports history, counting down from ten.

And number one, again: Bob Beamon, Mexico City, 1968. One jump, eight ninety. Fifty-five centimetres beyond the world record, and nobody jumped further for twenty-three years.

Darryl Sittler scored ten points in one game in 1976. It's still the NHL record.

But around it, everyone else's best night was usually seven points, and six times it was eight. Ten is about four steps: sixteenth, not top ten.

Goals, home runs and hits come in ones. When most seasons' best night is the same small number, one more can look enormous, or like nothing. So this list keeps the stats that come in big numbers.

So how does the score work? Picture a class test. Most of the class scores around seventy, give or take about five points.

Call that usual give-or-take one step.

A student who scores ninety-five is twenty-five points above the class average. That's five steps, so on our scale it's a score of five.

Put the same ninety-five in a class that averages ninety, and it's one step above: a score of one.

Same number, different class. Every day in this film was graded against the class of its own era: the best day by anyone else in each of the fifteen seasons before and after.

Three rules keep it fair. First, inflation. When every team scores more, each point is worth a little less, so in team sports everyone else's games are converted into the athlete's own season before they're compared.

Second, you're graded against your rivals, not yourself. Otherwise Wilt's hundred would be graded against Wilt's seventy-eight.

Third, one entry each, and only stats that come in big numbers.

The same method, two questions. Over a season, consistency stands out: Gretzky, DiMaggio, Ruth, Curry. Over a single game, team sports are noisy, and only Wilt's hundred and Kobe's eighty-one clear the bar.

So this isn't a ranking of the greatest athletes. It's how far one day stood from everyone else of its time. Which would you choose: the season, or the day?
