Out of Reach

How far each record sits above what today's best can do, how each event has moved over the years, and which single season, adjusted for its era, was the greatest performance of all.

Every record, one chart each

Each chart has two lines. The gold line is the record: it steps up every time the record is broken. The blue line is a typical world-class performance: the middle of each season's top ten, smoothed over five seasons. Where blue keeps climbing toward gold, the event is still improving. Where blue goes flat far below gold, the event has stalled and the record stands apart.

The number under each chart answers one question: at today's level, how often would the best athlete of a season match this record? It comes from the leading marks of the last 15 seasons.

The big four

The same charts for 20 of the best-known records in baseball, basketball, hockey and football. The blue line is the middle of each season's official top ten. For totals such as home runs or goals, the number under each chart compares the record with today's leaders at today's schedule, because seasons used to be shorter.

Baseball counts the American and National Leagues since 1901. Major League Baseball now also recognises the Negro Leagues of 1920–48, whose short schedules make season leaderboards hard to compare; with them, the batting average record is Josh Gibson's .466 (1943). Football's top ten is available from 1999, so its blue line starts there; sacks became an official statistic in 1982. Seasons cut short by strikes, lockouts or the pandemic are left out of the blue line and the numbers.

The greatest performance of all time

Records compete with all of history, so a record from 1904 meets today's very different game. A fairer test measures a season against its own time. For every season-leading performance in these 54 categories, we took the best of everyone else in the 15 seasons around it, with the athlete's own seasons left out, and asked the same question: how often would anyone else of that era have done this?

The record is not always the greatest performance

In many categories the most extraordinary season, judged against its own time, is not the one that holds the record.

Deeper dives

The athletics records in detail

Three kinds of record

For every event we compared the world record with what the world's best produce now: each season's leading mark from 2012 to 2026. From the spread of those leads we estimate how often a mark as good as the record would turn up at today's level. Read the results as orders of magnitude. A record that would come up less than once in 35 seasons, longer than any career, counts as out of reach.

How rare is the record today?

Each dot is a world record, placed by how often a mark that good would turn up at the current level of the event. The further right, the further the record sits above today's best.

The athlete or the era?

A record can stand apart for two reasons. One athlete may have gone far beyond everyone of their time. Or a whole generation was stronger than today's, and the record is simply that generation's best. Comparing each record with the four seasons before it, and with the last ten, separates the two.

Lines that rise belong to events whose field has fallen back since the record was set. Lines that fall belong to events where today's athletes have closed some of the distance.

Who is still chasing

Depth tells the story the record cannot. Here is how many athletes reached a fixed standard in each season since 1976. In every running event the field is deeper now than it has ever been. In the women's throws and long jump, and the men's high jump and hammer, the deepest seasons came in the 1980s.

When the best marks were set

Take the 100 best athletes in each event's history and note the decade in which each set their best mark. Every running event's list is led by the 2020s. The women's shot put and discus are led by the 1980s. The men's high jump, triple jump and hammer have barely added to their lists this decade.

Spotlight

The men's long jump

Mike Powell jumped 8.95 m in Tokyo on 30 August 1991, beating Carl Lewis in the same final. Since that night the best legal jump in the world has been 8.74 m, 21 cm short. The event did not stop producing great jumpers. It stopped producing the kind of jumps that were common for about fifteen years around Lewis and Powell.

What the numbers suggest

Speed. Biomechanists filmed the 1991 final. Lewis reached 11.06 m/s on the runway and Powell 11.00 m/s; the other finalists ran 10.20 to 10.58 m/s. A jump's length grows roughly with the square of take-off speed, so a few percent of speed is worth twice as much in distance. The 8.80 m jumps came from the fastest approaches ever measured in the event.

Where the speed went. Sprinting has never been deeper: 45 men broke 10.00 s for 100 m in 2026, against none to three a season in the 1980s. Very little of that speed reaches the long jump runway. Only 12 men in history have both jumped 8.20 m and run 10.09 s, and Lewis (9.86 and 8.87) is the only one near the top of both lists.

Beamon and altitude. Bob Beamon's 8.90 m in Mexico City (2,240 m, wind +2.0 m/s) added 55 cm to the record, 6.6% beyond the best marks of the four seasons before. The largest published estimate of the help from thin air and wind is about 31 cm (Ward-Smith, 1986); later measurements of wind effects in real competition found them smaller than that model predicted. Even with the full 31 cm removed, 8.59 m would have broken the old record by 24 cm.

Wind. Only one jump in history has gone farther than 8.95 m, and it does not count: Powell's own 8.99 m in Sestriere in 1992, at altitude with a +4.4 m/s tailwind. The longest wind-aided jumps since are Juan Miguel Echevarría's 8.92 m (+3.3, Havana, 2019) and 8.83 m (+2.1, Stockholm, 2018), just over the legal limit of +2.0.

The other standouts, in context

Each of these records is ratified and stands in World Athletics' books. The notes below describe conditions and eras, not individuals.

Lone outliers · 2009

Men's 100 m and 200 m

Usain Bolt's 9.58 and 19.19 stand in the deepest sprint fields there have ever been. At today's level a 9.58 would come up about once in 240 seasons. The closest anyone has come is 9.69, by Tyson Gay (2009) and Yohan Blake (2012); the best of 2017–2026 is Kishane Thompson's 9.75 (2025).

Lone outlier · 1988

Women's 100 m

Florence Griffith Joyner's 10.49 was ratified and stands. The wind gauge read 0.0 m/s while the triple-jump gauge nearby read over 4 m/s. A 1995 report for the IAAF by Nicholas Linthorne found the 100 m gauge misaligned by about 60° and estimated the wind at +5 to +7 m/s. Peter Larsson's all-time list files the mark under "doubtful wind reading". Her 10.61 in the final the next day (+1.2 m/s) is uncontested and ranks joint third in history.

Era records · 1987–88

Women's shot put, discus and long jump

These are the furthest from today's level of any athletics records: about once in 1,500 seasons for the shot, a few hundred for the discus and long jump. The depth of those seasons has not returned. 23 women put the shot 20 m in 1987; four to six do now. No woman threw the discus 68 m in 2008–2011. The marks predate random out-of-competition testing (late 1980s), stored samples for re-testing (2005) and the Athlete Biological Passport (2009).

Within reach again · 1983–88

Women's 200 m, 400 m and 800 m

Three records from the same era are now being approached, in events whose fields are deeper than ever. Sydney McLaughlin-Levrone ran 47.78 for 400 m in Tokyo in 2025, 0.18 s from Marita Koch's 47.60. Audrey Werro ran 1:53.70 for 800 m in Zürich in 2026, 0.42 s from Jarmila Kratochvílová's 1:53.28. Shericka Jackson's 21.41 (2023) is 0.07 s from 21.34.

Era record · 1993

Men's high jump

Javier Sotomayor's 2.45 m came at the end of the event's deepest stretch. 13 men cleared 2.35 m in 1988; one to three a season have done so since 2024. Mutaz Essa Barshim's 2.43 m (2014) is the closest anyone has come.

Era records · 1986 and 1996

Men's hammer and javelin

Yuriy Sedykh's 86.74 m hammer record dates from a year when Sergey Litvinov also threw 86.04 m. Throws of 80 m peaked at 23 men in 1988, fell to one or two a season in 2015–2020, and are recovering (11 in 2026; Ethan Katzberg's 84.70 m in 2025). Jan Železný's 98.48 m javelin record (1996) is the oldest set with the current implement; Johannes Vetter came within 72 cm of it in 2020.

Rules, testing and equipment

The changes that frame these records, in order.

  1. 1968The Mexico City Games are held at 2,240 m. Thin air helps sprints and horizontal jumps.
  2. 1977Electronic timing becomes compulsory for sprint world records.
  3. 1986The men's javelin is redesigned to fly shorter, and its records restart.
  4. Late 1980sRandom out-of-competition drug testing begins in athletics.
  5. 1999The women's javelin is redesigned, and its records restart.
  6. 2005Samples are stored so they can be re-tested later.
  7. 2009The IAAF adds the Athlete Biological Passport to its testing programme (August).
  8. 2017European Athletics endorses a proposal to recognise only records backed by stored samples and sends it to the IAAF. The world record list is not reset.
  9. 2020Shoe rules limit the stack height of track spikes (20 mm up to 400 m, 25 mm from 800 m).
  10. 2024A single 20 mm limit applies to all track spikes from 1 November.

How this was measured

  • Marks. Every legal performance down to a deep cut-off, from Peter Larsson's all-time lists (alltime-athletics.com, read November 2026): 163,664 legal performances across the 34 events. Running events use fully automatic, legal-wind marks. Jumps and throws include indoor marks, which count for world records. Wind-aided marks are used only as context.
  • Seasons. Calendar years. Because the lists are complete above their cut-offs, each season's leading mark and its count of athletes beyond a standard are exact. Where a season's 10th-best athlete falls below the cut-off, the depth is shown as below it.
  • Performance. Speed for running events, distance or height for jumps and throws, so a percentage means the same in every event. Jumps and throws grow roughly with the square of speed, so their percentages run about twice those of running events; the return periods do not depend on this.
  • Return periods. A Gumbel distribution fitted by moments to the season-leading marks of 2012–2026, then the chance that a season's best reaches the record. Real tails are often lighter than Gumbel's, so the largest figures, if anything, understate how rare the record is. Read them as orders of magnitude.
  • Groups. Out of reach means a return period of 35 seasons or more. An era record is an out-of-reach record set by 2000, in an event whose depth (the season's 10th-best athlete, five-season averages) peaked before 2006 and is still below that peak. A lone outlier is an out-of-reach record in an event whose field is as deep as it has been.
  • Records. The world record lines from The Record and the Rest, built from World Athletics' progressions via Wikipedia, including 2026 records pending ratification.
  • The blue lines. The median of each season's ten best athletes (athletics: each athlete counted once; leagues: the official leaders), averaged over five seasons centred on each one. In athletics the line starts in the first season in which the all-time lists hold ten athletes (1968–1999, by event); World Athletics' season lists fill the ranks below the all-time lists' cut-offs from 2001. Seasons cut short (strikes, lockouts, the 2020 pandemic season) are left out.
  • The big four. Season leaders from Baseball-Reference (American and National Leagues since 1901, with the AL and NL leader pages for batting average and ERA in 1920–48), Basketball-Reference and Hockey-Reference; football leaders from Wikipedia's annual-leaders lists (NFL, with the AFL in 1960–69) and top tens from nflverse (1999–2025). Hitting streaks were computed game by game from Retrosheet's logs (via Chadwick's retrosplits), following the scoring rule: a game with a hit extends a streak, a game with an at-bat or a sacrifice fly and no hit ends it, and a game of only walks, hit-by-pitches or sacrifice bunts leaves it alone. The computed streaks match the record books (DiMaggio 56, Rose 44, Sisler 41, Cobb 40).
  • Today's schedule. For totals (home runs, goals, yards) each recent season's leader is scaled to today's schedule before fitting, and so is the record (for example Gretzky's 92 goals in 80 games become 94 in 82). Rates such as batting average or points per game are not scaled.
  • Era-adjusted. For each season-leading performance: the best of everyone else, season by season, in the 15 seasons centred on it, with the performer's own marks left out and totals scaled to that season's schedule. The same Gumbel fit gives "once in N seasons of its era"; the ranking uses the equivalent number of standard deviations. Athletics performances count from 1960, when the all-time lists become deep enough; a window needs eight seasons with real values. Windows of 11 and 21 seasons give the same leader (Beamon), and Gretzky, Chamberlain and Rojas stay in the top ten under all three.
