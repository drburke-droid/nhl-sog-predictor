# Narration script: The 10 Biggest Single Performances in Athletics and Swimming

Timecodes are positions in the final cut (11:00). Each card is 36 seconds. Never say "proved", "greatest athlete" or
"certain". Every number here is on screen or in facts_b.json.

## Opening: why eras matter, and how the score allows for them (0:00)

**0:01** Athletes keep getting better. Since 1950, world records in athletics and swimming have improved by about twenty-three percent in the typical event.

**0:10** Science, nutrition, full-time training and better technique: every generation starts from a higher floor.

**0:20** A sprinter of 1950, with little weight training, sports science or modern technique, can't be compared with a full-time professional of today without allowing for it.

**0:31** In 1950 the hundred-metre world record was ten point two. Today it's nine fifty-eight.

**0:37** In athletics and swimming a metre is a metre, so nothing is converted.

**0:44** Instead, each mark is compared with the best by everyone else in the fifteen seasons before and after. They trained and competed with much the same science, so together they set the standard for the best of their time: the curve.

**0:59** The usual spread of the best around that curve is one standard deviation. The score is how many standard deviations a performance sits above it.

**1:07** Katie Ledecky's eight oh four seventy-nine in 2016 is almost five standard deviations above the curve.

**1:17** The method also handles a harder case. Two of the oldest world records are in the women's throws: the shot put from 1987 and the discus from 1988.

**1:29** In the 1980s, marks like these were routine at the top: eighteen shot puts of twenty-two metres or more, and fifty discus throws of seventy-two or more. No woman has put the shot twenty-two metres since 1988.

**1:43** So the curve for those years sits high, and the records stand only two point three and three point two standard deviations above it. The whole era is discounted, without singling anyone out.

## How the score works: grading on a curve (8:18)

**8:20** Here's the same idea as a class test. Most of the class scores around seventy, give or take about
five points.

**8:28** Call that usual give-or-take one step.

**8:34** A student who scores ninety-five is twenty-five points above the class average: five steps. A score of five.

**8:45** Put the same ninety-five in a class that averages ninety, and it's one step above: a score of one.

**8:54** Same number, different class. So every mark is graded against its own era.

## Four rules that keep it fair (9:02)

**9:03** Four rules keep it fair. First, you're graded against your rivals, not yourself.

**9:11** Second, one entry each: an athlete's highest-scoring mark.

**9:19** Third, the supersuit years. In 2008 and 2009 swimmers raced in polyurethane suits, and those two seasons are
treated as a shift.

**9:28** And fourth, a mark must stand at least one step clear of every other mark on its graph. Katie Ledecky's eight
oh four in 2016 isn't: Summer McIntosh's eight oh five, in 2025, comes within a step.
