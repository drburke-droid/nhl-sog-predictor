## Opening: why eras matter, and how the score allows for them (0:00)

**0:01.0** Athletes keep getting better... Since 1950, world records in athletics and swimming have improved by about twenty-three percent.

**0:10.0** Science, nutrition, full-time training and better technique let every generation start from a higher floor.

**0:20.0** The games themselves also change... For example, four home runs by one player in a single game in 1932, when whole teams were only hitting about one every other game, is much more impressive than the same feat in 2017, when teams were hitting more than twice as many home runs.

**0:39.0** In the same way, an athlete of the 1950s, with little weight training or nutrition science, can't be fairly compared with today's full-time professionals, who build every part of their day around their sport, unless we allow for it.

**0:54.0** So we look for the biggest statistical outliers in two steps. Step one: every performance is converted to the scoring level of the season being judged.

**1:05.0** In hockey's run-and-gun 1980s, teams scored almost four goals a game. In 2003–04, about two and a half. We need to adjust the data to account for this.

**1:18.0** Step two: each game is compared with the five best games by everyone else, in each of the fifteen seasons before and after.

**1:26.0** Their peers in this group trained and competed with much the same science, so together they set the standard for the best of their time: the curve.

**1:36.0** The usual spread of the best around that curve is one standard deviation. The measure we use to find the greatest performances is how many standard deviations a single performance sits above it.

**1:49.0** On a bell curve, about sixty-eight percent of results fall within one standard deviation either side, ninety-five percent within two, and ninety-nine point seven percent within three.

**2:02.0** Adrian Peterson's record two hundred and ninety-six rushing yards stand almost four standard deviations above the curve. On a perfect bell curve, a result that high would come up about once in twenty thousand tries.

## Hook (2:16.4)

**2:17.4** I analysed four thousand three hundred and sixty-four season-leading performances in basketball, hockey, baseball and football.

**2:25.3** Each was graded against the best games of everyone else in its era: fifteen years before and, where possible, fifteen years after.

**2:33.0** Most performances land close to the curve. That makes sense: the curve is the typical level of the best in each era.

**2:40.5** Only six are more than five standard deviations above it. On a perfect bell curve, that's about one in three and a half million.

**2:48.9** Ten stood clear of the field. So without further introduction, these are the ten biggest single-game outliers in team sports, counting down from number ten.

## #10 Derrick Thomas, 1990 (3:00.3)

**3:01.9** Number ten. Derrick Thomas, Kansas City, against Seattle in 1990: seven sacks in one game.

**3:07.9** Each dot is one of the five best sack games by anyone else that season, fifteen years either side, converted to 1990's sack rate. The line through them is the curve. Each tick above it is one standard deviation.

**3:21.9** Seven sacks is four point three three standard deviations above the curve, and it's still the NFL record.

## #9 Bert Olmstead, 1953–54 (3:35.7)

**3:36.9** Number nine. Bert Olmstead, Montreal, January 1954: eight points in one game, four goals and four assists.

**3:47.9** It matched Maurice Richard's record from 1944. Converted to 1954's lower scoring, Richard's eight is worth about six and three-quarters.

**3:59.9** The next best that season was five points.

## #8 Darryl Sittler, 1975–76 (4:11.1)

**4:12.9** Number eight. Darryl Sittler, Toronto, against Boston in 1976: ten points, six goals and four assists.

**4:22.9** The curve for this era sits near six points, and one standard deviation is about three-quarters of a point. Ten points is nearly five standard deviations higher.

**4:35.7** It's still the NHL record.

## #7 Mark Whiten, 1993 (4:46.5)

**4:47.9** Number seven. Mark Whiten, St. Louis, at Cincinnati in 1993: four home runs and twelve runs batted in.

**4:57.9** It came in the second game of a doubleheader, and it tied a record set in 1924.

**5:06.9** The next best that season was eight RBI.

## #6 Bob Cousy, 1958–59 (5:21.9)

**5:23.9** Number six. Bob Cousy, Boston, February 1959: twenty-eight assists in one game.

**5:31.9** Guy Rodgers matched it in 1963, when teams were passing more. In 1959 terms, though, Rodgers's twenty-eight equates to about twenty-five.

**5:42.9** Kevin Porter's twenty-nine broke the record in 1978.

## #5 Cloyce Box, 1950 (5:57.3)

**5:58.9** Number five. Cloyce Box, Detroit, at Baltimore in 1950: three hundred and two receiving yards.

**6:07.9** The curve is about a hundred and seventy yards, and one standard deviation is about twenty-five. Box is more than five standard deviations above it.

**6:19.7** Football's box scores here start in 1950, so this game is compared only with the seasons after it.

## #4 Jim Bottomley, 1924 (6:32.7)

**6:33.9** Number four. Jim Bottomley, St. Louis, at Brooklyn in 1924: twelve runs batted in.

**6:42.9** Six hits, two of them home runs. Most seasons, the best games by anyone else drove in six or seven.

**6:54.9** Twelve is still the major-league record. Mark Whiten matched it in 1993.

## #3 Norm Van Brocklin, 1951 (7:08.1)

**7:09.9** Number three. Norm Van Brocklin, Los Angeles, against the New York Yanks in 1951: five hundred and fifty-four passing yards.

**7:20.9** Passing has grown since. Converted to 1951's rate, the closest anyone has come is Y.A. Tittle's five hundred and five in 1962, which converts to about four hundred and seventy in 1951.

**7:34.1** It's still the NFL record.

## #2 Kobe Bryant, 2005–06 (7:43.5)

**7:44.9** Number two. Kobe Bryant, against Toronto in January 2006: eighty-one points, fifty-five of them in the second half.

**7:55.9** The curve is about fifty-one points, and one standard deviation is about four and a half. Kobe is nearly seven standard deviations higher.

**8:06.9** Only one player has scored more in an NBA game.

## #1 Wilt Chamberlain, 1961–62 (8:18.9)

**8:20.9** Number one. Wilt Chamberlain, Philadelphia, 2 March 1962: one hundred points.

**8:29.9** The next best that season was sixty-three. Teams averaged almost a hundred and nineteen points a game, and the score allows for it.

**8:40.9** It is still almost nine standard deviations above the curve: the biggest single-game outlier in team sports history.

## How the score works: grading on a curve (8:54.3)

**8:55.3** Here's the same idea, using a class test. One student scores ninety-five. Leave them out, and take the five best scores by everyone else in the class: they're all around eighty.

**9:06.5** Do the same for the fifteen classes before and the fifteen after. Their top fives land around eighty too, and the line through them is the curve.

**9:14.8** If one year's test was easier for everyone, its scores are scaled down first, just as every season is converted to the same scoring level.

**9:22.2** The top scores usually sit within about three points of the curve. That's one standard deviation.

**9:28.9** So the student's ninety-five is fifteen points above the curve: five standard deviations.

**9:35.0** But if the top fives in those years had scored around ninety-two, the same ninety-five would be just one standard deviation up.

**9:42.4** Same score, different era. So every game is graded against the best of everyone else in its own era.

## Four rules that keep it fair (9:49.8)

**9:51.0** Four rules keep it fair. First, every game is scaled to regulation length: overtime and extra innings are extra chances.

**9:59.0** Second, inflation: other seasons' games are converted into the athlete's season, by at most one and a half times.

**10:08.0** Third, the class is the five best games by anyone else each season, and a count is measured from half a unit below.

**10:16.0** And fourth, a game must stand at least one standard deviation clear of every other game on its graph. Calvin Johnson's three hundred and twenty-nine yards come within one standard deviation of Jimmy Smith's game in 2000, so they're left out.
