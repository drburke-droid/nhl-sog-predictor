# Narration script: The 10 Biggest Single-Game Outliers in Team Sports

Timecodes are positions in the final cut (10:44). Each card is 36 seconds. Never say "proved", "greatest athlete" or
"certain". Every number here is on screen or in facts_a.json.

## Opening: why eras matter, and how the score allows for them (0:00)

**0:01** Athletes keep getting better. Since 1950, world records in athletics and swimming have improved by about twenty-three percent in the typical event.

**0:11** Science, nutrition, full-time training and better technique: every generation starts from a higher floor.

**0:20** And the game itself changes. Four home runs in a game in 1932, when teams hit one about every other game, isn't the same feat as in 2017, when they hit more than one a game.

**0:32** An athlete of the 1950s, with little weight training or nutrition science, can't be compared with today's full-time professionals without allowing for it.

**0:44** So we look for the biggest statistical outliers in two steps. Step one: every performance is converted to the scoring level of the season being judged.

**0:55** In hockey's run-and-gun 1980s, teams scored almost four goals a game. In 2003–04, about two and a half.

**1:04** Step two: each game is compared with the five best games by everyone else, in each of the fifteen seasons before and after.

**1:12** They trained and competed with much the same science, so together they set the standard for the best of their time: the curve.

**1:22** The usual spread of the best around that curve is one standard deviation. The score is how many standard deviations a performance sits above it.

**1:31** Adrian Peterson's record two hundred and ninety-six rushing yards stand almost four standard deviations above the curve.

## #10 Derrick Thomas, 1990 (2:08)

**2:10** Number ten. Derrick Thomas, Kansas City, against Seattle in 1990: seven sacks in one game.

**2:16** Each dot is one of the five best games by anyone else that season, fifteen years either side, converted to 1990's
sack rate. The line through them is the curve. Each tick above it is one step.

**2:30** A count of seven stands for anything from six and a half to seven and a half, so it's measured from six and a
half. It's still the NFL record.

## #9 Bert Olmstead, 1953–54 (2:44)

**2:45** Nine. Bert Olmstead, Montreal, January 1954: eight points in one game, four goals and four assists.

**2:56** It matched Maurice Richard's record from 1944. Converted to 1954's lower scoring, Richard's eight is about six
and three-quarters.

**3:08** The next best that season was five points.

## #8 Darryl Sittler, 1975–76 (3:19)

**3:21** Eight. Darryl Sittler, Toronto, against Boston in 1976: ten points, six goals and four assists.

**3:31** The curve sits near six points, and one step is under three-quarters of a point. Ten is nearly five steps up.

**3:43** It's still the NHL record.

## #7 Mark Whiten, 1993 (3:55)

**3:56** Seven. Mark Whiten, St. Louis, at Cincinnati in 1993: four home runs and twelve runs batted in.

**4:06** It came in the second game of a doubleheader, and it tied a record set in 1924.

**4:15** The next best that season was eight.

## #6 Bob Cousy, 1958–59 (4:30)

**4:32** Six. Bob Cousy, Boston, February 1959: twenty-eight assists.

**4:40** Guy Rodgers matched it in 1963, when teams passed more. In 1959 terms, that's about twenty-five.

**4:51** Kevin Porter's twenty-nine broke the record in 1978.

## #5 Cloyce Box, 1950 (5:05)

**5:07** Five. Cloyce Box, Detroit, at Baltimore in 1950: three hundred and two receiving yards.

**5:16** The curve is about a hundred and seventy yards, and one step is about twenty-five. Box is more than five steps

## #4 Jim Bottomley, 1924 (5:41)

**5:42** Four. Jim Bottomley, St. Louis, at Brooklyn in 1924: twelve runs batted in.

**5:51** Six hits, two of them home runs. Most seasons, the best games by anyone else drove in six or seven.

**6:03** Twelve is still the major-league record. Mark Whiten matched it in 1993.

**6:41** It's still the NFL record.

**6:53** Two. Kobe Bryant, against Toronto in January 2006: eighty-one points, fifty-five of them in the second half.

**7:04** The curve is about fifty-one points, and one step about four and a half. Kobe is nearly seven steps up.

**7:15** Only one player has scored more in an NBA game.

## #1 Wilt Chamberlain, 1961–62 (7:27)

**7:29** Number one. Wilt Chamberlain, Philadelphia, 2 March 1962: one hundred points.

**7:38** The next best that season was sixty-three. Teams averaged almost a hundred and nineteen points a game, and the
score allows for it.

**7:49** It is still almost nine steps above the curve: the biggest single-game outlier in team sports.

## How the score works: grading on a curve (8:02)

**8:04** Here's the same idea as a class test. Most of the class scores around seventy, give or take about
five points.

**8:12** Call that usual give-or-take one step.

**8:18** A student who scores ninety-five is twenty-five points above the class average: five steps. A score of five.

**8:29** Put the same ninety-five in a class that averages ninety, and it's one step above: a score of one.

**8:38** Same number, different class. So every game is graded against its own era.

## Four rules that keep it fair (8:46)

**8:47** Four rules keep it fair. First, every game is scaled to regulation length: overtime and extra innings are extra
chances.

**8:55** Second, inflation: other seasons' games are converted into the athlete's season, by at most one and a half times.

**9:04** Third, the class is the five best games by anyone else each season, and a count is measured from half a unit
below.

**9:12** And fourth, a game must stand at least one step clear of every other game on its graph. Calvin Johnson's three
hundred and twenty-nine yards come within a step of Jimmy Smith's 2000, so they're left out.
