# Narration script: The 10 Biggest Single-Game Outliers in Team Sports

The script from the countdown on, for script_a.py. Each cue is "**+s**": seconds after its section starts (a line
that would run into the next pushes it later). The opening, the hook and "grading on a curve" are timed to their
narration in script_a.py; this file holds the sections of fixed length (each card is 36 seconds). Never say
"proved", "greatest athlete" or "certain". Every number here is on screen or in facts_a.json.

## c10 · #10 Derrick Thomas, 1990

**+1.6** Number ten. Derrick Thomas, Kansas City, against Seattle in 1990: seven sacks in one game.

**+7.6** Each dot is one of the five best sack games by anyone else that season, fifteen years either side, converted
to 1990's sack rate. The line through them is the curve. Each tick above it is one standard deviation.

**+21.6** Seven sacks is four point three three standard deviations above the curve, and it's still the NFL record.

## c9 · #9 Bert Olmstead, 1953–54

**+1.2** Number nine. Bert Olmstead, Montreal, January 1954: eight points in one game, four goals and four assists.

**+12.2** It matched Maurice Richard's record from 1944. Converted to 1954's lower scoring, Richard's eight is worth
about six and three-quarters.

**+24.2** The next best that season was five points.

## c8 · #8 Darryl Sittler, 1975–76

**+1.8** Number eight. Darryl Sittler, Toronto, against Boston in 1976: ten points, six goals and four assists.

**+11.8** The curve for this era sits near six points, and one standard deviation is about three-quarters of a point.
Ten points is nearly five standard deviations higher.

**+24.6** It's still the NHL record.

## c7 · #7 Mark Whiten, 1993

**+1.4** Number seven. Mark Whiten, St. Louis, at Cincinnati in 1993: four home runs and twelve runs batted in.

**+11.4** It came in the second game of a doubleheader, and it tied a record set in 1924.

**+20.4** The next best that season was eight RBI.

## c6 · #6 Bob Cousy, 1958–59

**+2.0** Number six. Bob Cousy, Boston, February 1959: twenty-eight assists in one game.

**+10.0** Guy Rodgers matched it in 1963, when teams were passing more. In 1959 terms, though, Rodgers's twenty-eight
equates to about twenty-five.

**+21.0** Kevin Porter's twenty-nine broke the record in 1978.

## c5 · #5 Cloyce Box, 1950

**+1.6** Number five. Cloyce Box, Detroit, at Baltimore in 1950: three hundred and two receiving yards.

**+10.6** The curve is about a hundred and seventy yards, and one standard deviation is about twenty-five. Box is more
than five standard deviations above it.

**+22.4** Football's box scores here start in 1950, so this game is compared only with the seasons after it.

## c4 · #4 Jim Bottomley, 1924

**+1.2** Number four. Jim Bottomley, St. Louis, at Brooklyn in 1924: twelve runs batted in.

**+10.2** Six hits, two of them home runs. Most seasons, the best games by anyone else drove in six or seven.

**+22.2** Twelve is still the major-league record. Mark Whiten matched it in 1993.

## c3 · #3 Norm Van Brocklin, 1951

**+1.8** Number three. Norm Van Brocklin, Los Angeles, against the New York Yanks in 1951: five hundred and fifty-four
passing yards.

**+12.8** Passing has grown since. Converted to 1951's rate, the closest anyone has come is Y.A. Tittle's five hundred
and five in 1962, which converts to about four hundred and seventy in 1951.

**+26.0** It's still the NFL record.

## c2 · #2 Kobe Bryant, 2005–06

**+1.4** Number two. Kobe Bryant, against Toronto in January 2006: eighty-one points, fifty-five of them in the second
half.

**+12.4** The curve is about fifty-one points, and one standard deviation is about four and a half. Kobe is nearly seven
standard deviations higher.

**+23.4** Only one player has scored more in an NBA game.

## c1 · #1 Wilt Chamberlain, 1961–62

**+2.0** Number one. Wilt Chamberlain, Philadelphia, 2 March 1962: one hundred points.

**+11.0** The next best that season was sixty-three. Teams averaged almost a hundred and nineteen points a game, and the
score allows for it.

**+22.0** It is still almost nine standard deviations above the curve: the biggest single-game outlier in team sports
history.

## fair · Four rules that keep it fair

**+1.2** Four rules keep it fair. First, every game is scaled to regulation length: overtime and extra innings are
extra chances.

**+9.2** Second, inflation: other seasons' games are converted into the athlete's season, by at most one and a half
times.

**+18.2** Third, the class is the five best games by anyone else each season, and a count is measured from half a unit
below.

**+26.2** And fourth, a game must stand at least one standard deviation clear of every other game on its graph. Calvin
Johnson's three hundred and twenty-nine yards come within one standard deviation of Jimmy Smith's game in 2000, so
they're left out.

## sure · How sure is this list?

**+1.8** How sure is this list? We ranked every game sixteen ways: different comparison windows, straight or curved

**+13.8** Four made the top ten in all sixteen. Eight made at least twelve.

**+23.8** Cousy's twenty-eight and Olmstead's eight depend most on the choices.
