# Narration script: The 5 Biggest Season-Long Outliers in Team Sports

The script from the countdown on, for script_c.py. Each cue is "**+s**": seconds after its section starts (a line
that would run into the next pushes it later). The opening, the hook and grading on a curve are timed to their
narration (opening.py, script_c.py); this file holds the sections of fixed length (each card is 36 seconds). Never say "proved",
"greatest athlete" or "certain". Every number here is on screen or in facts_c.json.

## c5 · #5 Ichiro Suzuki, 2004

**+1.2** Number five. Ichiro Suzuki, Seattle, 2004: two hundred and sixty-two hits.

**+7.2** Each bin holds the most hits by anyone else that season, fifteen years either side, converted to 2004's league hit rate. The line is the elite curve. Each tick above it is one standard deviation.

**+20.2** It broke George Sisler's record of two hundred and fifty-seven, set in 1920. The next best in 2004 had two hundred and twenty-one.

## c4 · #4 Dennis Rodman, 1992–93

**+0.8** Four. Dennis Rodman, Detroit, 1992–93: eighteen point three rebounds a game.

**+9.8** The elite curve sits near thirteen. Allowing for how many rebounds the whole league grabbed, Rodman's season is more than five and a half standard deviations above it.

**+21.8** He led the league in rebounding seven seasons running. This is the one furthest from everyone else.

## c3 · #3 Wayne Gretzky, 1985–86

**+1.4** Three. Wayne Gretzky, Edmonton, 1985–86: one hundred and sixty-three assists.

**+10.4** His assists alone were more than any other player's points that season.

**+18.4** Scoring was near its peak, so the league adjustment brings his score down, to six point four seven. It made the top ten in all sixteen versions of the method.

## c2 · #2 Joe DiMaggio, 1941

**+1.0** Two. Joe DiMaggio, New York, 1941: a hit in fifty-six games in a row.

**+10.0** Most seasons, the longest streak by anyone else is in the twenties. One standard deviation is about four games, and DiMaggio is seven standard deviations up.

**+22.0** The closest anyone has come since: Pete Rose's forty-four, in 1978.

## c1 · #1 Wilt Chamberlain, 1961–62

**+1.6** Number one. Wilt Chamberlain, Philadelphia, 1961–62: fifty point four points a game.

**+10.6** The next best scorer that season, Walt Bellamy, averaged thirty-one point six.

**+18.6** Teams scored a lot in 1962, and the score allows for it. Even so, Wilt's season stands seven point oh five standard deviations above the curve: the biggest season-long outlier in team sports.

## fair · Four rules that keep it fair

**+0.8** Four rules keep it fair. First, inflation: when every team scores more, each point is worth a little less, so other seasons are converted into the athlete's own season.

**+11.8** Second, you're graded against your rivals, not yourself.

**+18.8** Third, one entry each: an athlete's highest-scoring season.

**+26.8** And fourth, a season must stand at least one standard deviation clear of every other season on its graph. Stephen Curry's four hundred and two threes score five point six nine, but Ray Allen's 2006 comes within one standard deviation, so Curry is left out.

## sure · How sure is this list?

**+1.4** How sure is this list? We ranked every season sixteen ways: different comparison windows, straight or curved trends, with or without the league adjustment.

**+13.4** Wilt, DiMaggio and Gretzky made the top ten in all sixteen. Rodman and Ichiro, in at least twelve. Those are the five.

**+23.4** The other five in the top ten by score each rest on one choice of method.

## considered · Five that came close

**+2.4** Dazzy Vance's two hundred and sixty-two strikeouts in 1924 rest on the league adjustment. Without it, the bigger totals of Rube Waddell and Walter Johnson win.

**+12.8** So do Babe Ruth's fifty-four home runs in 1920: without the adjustment, the home-run boom he started catches him.

**+22.8** Bob Feller's three hundred and forty-eight strikeouts in 1946 slip out of the ten with shorter comparison windows.

**+32.8** Without the adjustment, Maury Wills's hundred and four steals are overtaken by Lou Brock and Rickey Henderson.

**+42.8** And Ted Williams's four oh six: without the adjustment, Rogers Hornsby and Bill Terry come within one standard deviation.
