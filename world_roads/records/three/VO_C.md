# Narration script: The 5 Biggest Season-Long Outliers in Team Sports

Timecodes are positions in the final cut (6:59). Each card is 36 seconds. Never say "proved", "greatest athlete" or
"certain". Every number here is on screen or in facts_c.json.

## Hook (0:00–0:30)

**0:01** Two thousand six hundred and seven season-leading performances, in basketball, hockey, baseball and football.

**0:07** Each one graded against everyone else of its era.

**0:10** Most land near zero: leading your league is normal. Only ten reach five.

**0:17** Five stood clear of the field.

**0:23** These are the five biggest season-long outliers in team sports, counting down from five.

## #5 Ichiro Suzuki, 2004 (0:29)

**0:31** Number five. Ichiro Suzuki, Seattle, 2004: two hundred and sixty-two hits.

**0:37** Each bin holds the most hits by anyone else that season, fifteen years either side, converted to 2004's league
hit rate. The line is the curve. Each tick above it is one step.

**0:50** It broke George Sisler's record of two hundred and fifty-seven, set in 1920. The next best in 2004 had two
hundred and twenty-one.

## #4 Dennis Rodman, 1992–93 (1:05)

**1:06** Four. Dennis Rodman, Detroit, 1992–93: eighteen point three rebounds a game.

**1:15** The curve sits near thirteen. Allowing for how many rebounds the whole league grabbed, Rodman's season is more
than five and a half steps above it.

**1:27** He led the league in rebounding seven seasons running. This is the one furthest from everyone else.

## #3 Wayne Gretzky, 1985–86 (1:40)

**1:42** Three. Wayne Gretzky, Edmonton, 1985–86: one hundred and sixty-three assists.

**1:51** His assists alone were more than any other player's points that season.

**1:59** Scoring was near its peak, so the league adjustment brings his score down, to six point four seven. It made
the top ten in all sixteen versions of the method.

## #2 Joe DiMaggio, 1941 (2:16)

**2:17** Two. Joe DiMaggio, New York, 1941: a hit in fifty-six games in a row.

**2:26** Most seasons, the longest streak by anyone else is in the twenties. One step is about four games, and
DiMaggio is seven steps up.

**2:38** The closest anyone has come since: Pete Rose's forty-four, in 1978.

## #1 Wilt Chamberlain, 1961–62 (2:51)

**2:53** Number one. Wilt Chamberlain, Philadelphia, 1961–62: fifty point four points a game.

**3:02** The next best scorer that season, Walt Bellamy, averaged thirty-one point six.

**3:10** Teams scored a lot in 1962, and the score allows for it. Even so, Wilt's season stands seven point oh five steps
above the curve: the biggest season-long outlier in team sports.

## How the score works: grading on a curve (3:26)

**3:28** How does the score work? Picture a class test. Most of the class scores around seventy, give or take about
five points.

**3:36** Call that usual give-or-take one step.

**3:42** A student who scores ninety-five is twenty-five points above the class average: five steps. A score of five.

**3:53** Put the same ninety-five in a class that averages ninety, and it's one step above: a score of one.

**4:02** Same number, different class. So every season is graded against its own era.

## Four rules that keep it fair (4:10)

**4:11** Four rules keep it fair. First, inflation: when every team scores more, each point is worth a little less, so
other seasons are converted into the athlete's own season.

**4:22** Second, you're graded against your rivals, not yourself.

**4:29** Third, one entry each: an athlete's highest-scoring season.

**4:37** And fourth, a season must stand at least one step clear of every other season on its graph. Stephen Curry's
four hundred and two threes score five point six nine, but Ray Allen's 2006 comes within a step, so Curry is left out.

## How sure is this list? (4:49)

**4:51** How sure is this list? We ranked every season sixteen ways: different comparison windows, straight or curved
trends, with or without the league adjustment.

**5:03** Wilt, DiMaggio and Gretzky made the top ten in all sixteen. Rodman and Ichiro, in at least twelve. Those are
the five.

**5:13** The other five in the top ten by score each rest on one choice of method.

## Five that came close (5:21)

**5:23** Dazzy Vance's two hundred and sixty-two strikeouts in 1924 rest on the league adjustment. Without it, the bigger
totals of Rube Waddell and Walter Johnson win.

**5:32** So do Babe Ruth's fifty-four home runs in 1920: without the adjustment, the home-run boom he started catches him.

**5:41** Bob Feller's three hundred and forty-eight strikeouts in 1946 slip out of the ten with shorter comparison
windows.

**5:50** Without the adjustment, Maury Wills's hundred and four steals are overtaken by Lou Brock and Rickey Henderson.

**5:59** And Ted Williams's four oh six: without the adjustment, Rogers Hornsby and Bill Terry come within a step.
