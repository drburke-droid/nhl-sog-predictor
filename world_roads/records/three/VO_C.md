# Narration script: The 5 Biggest Season-Long Outliers in Team Sports

Timecodes are positions in the final cut (9:50), in seconds and tenths. Written by script_c.py from opening.py (the opening), the hook's lines and VO_C_body.md (the rest). Names are spelled normally here; say.json respells a few for the voice. Never say "proved", "greatest athlete" or "certain".

## Opening: why eras matter, and how the score allows for them (0:00)

**0:01.0** Athletes keep getting better. Since 1950, world records in athletics and swimming have improved by about twenty-three percent in the typical event.

**0:11.0** Science, nutrition, full-time training and better technique: every generation starts from a higher floor.

**0:20.0** The games themselves also change. Sixty home runs in 1927, when teams hit one every three games, is a much more impressive feat than hitting seventy in 1998, when teams were hitting nearly three times as many.

**0:35.0** An athlete of the 1950s, with little weight training or nutrition science, can't be compared with today's full-time professionals without controlling for these changes.

**0:48.0** So I looked for the biggest statistical outliers in team sports history in two steps. Step one: every other performance is converted to the scoring level of the season being judged.

**1:00.0** For example, in hockey's run-and-gun 1980s, teams scored almost four goals a game. In 2003–2004, about two and a half.

**1:12.0** Step two: each season is compared with only the best of everyone else in the fifteen seasons before and after. Each of these players was in the top one percent of their league that season.

**1:24.0** They trained and competed with much the same science, so together they set the standard for the best of their time. Let's call that the elite curve.

**1:35.0** The usual spread of the best around that curve is one standard deviation. The score we assign our athletes is how many standard deviations a performance sits above it. About two thirds of these elite seasons sit within one standard deviation of the elite curve, and ninety-five percent within two.

**1:53.0** Barry Bonds's seventy-three home runs are the record, but only two standard deviations above the elite curve. Impressive for sure, but in his era, the other best hitters were that good. And two standard deviations doesn't crack the top of our list.

## Hook (2:07.4)

**2:08.4** I found and isolated two thousand six hundred and seven season-leading performances, in basketball, hockey, baseball and football, and set out to determine which ones were the most impressive.

**2:19.9** Each one graded against the best of its era.

**2:23.2** Most land near zero: right on the elite curve. That makes sense: these are the league leaders, the best in their sport.

**2:31.1** But ten landed five or more standard deviations above it.

**2:38.1** And five of them stood well clear of everyone else.

**2:41.3** These are the five biggest season-long outliers in team sports, counting down from five.

## #5 Ichiro Suzuki, 2004 (2:49.5)

**2:50.7** Number five. Ichiro Suzuki, Seattle, 2004: two hundred and sixty-two hits.

**2:56.7** Each bin holds the most hits by anyone else that season, fifteen years either side, converted to 2004's league hit rate. The line is the elite curve. Each tick above it is one standard deviation.

**3:09.7** It broke George Sisler's record of two hundred and fifty-seven, set in 1920. The next best in 2004 had two hundred and twenty-one.

## #4 Dennis Rodman, 1992–93 (3:24.9)

**3:25.7** Four. Dennis Rodman, Detroit, 1992–93: eighteen point three rebounds a game.

**3:34.7** The elite curve sits near thirteen. Allowing for how many rebounds the whole league grabbed, Rodman's season is more than five and a half standard deviations above it.

**3:46.7** He led the league in rebounding seven seasons running. This is the one furthest from everyone else.

## #3 Wayne Gretzky, 1985–86 (4:00.3)

**4:01.7** Three. Wayne Gretzky, Edmonton, 1985–86: one hundred and sixty-three assists.

**4:10.7** His assists alone were more than any other player's points that season.

**4:18.7** Scoring was near its peak, so the league adjustment brings his score down, to six point four seven. It made the top ten in all sixteen versions of the method.

## #2 Joe DiMaggio, 1941 (4:35.7)

**4:36.7** Two. Joe DiMaggio, New York, 1941: a hit in fifty-six games in a row.

**4:45.7** Most seasons, the longest streak by anyone else is in the twenties. One standard deviation is about four games, and DiMaggio is seven standard deviations up.

**4:57.7** The closest anyone has come since: Pete Rose's forty-four, in 1978.

## #1 Wilt Chamberlain, 1961–62 (5:11.1)

**5:12.7** Number one. Wilt Chamberlain, Philadelphia, 1961–62: fifty point four points a game.

**5:21.7** The next best scorer that season, Walt Bellamy, averaged thirty-one point six.

**5:29.7** Teams scored a lot in 1962, and the score allows for it. Even so, Wilt's season stands seven point oh five standard deviations above the curve: the biggest season-long outlier in team sports.

## How the score works: grading on a curve (5:46.5)

**5:47.5** Here's the same idea as a class test. One student scores ninety-five. Leave them out, and look at the rest of the class: its best score is about eighty.

**5:57.9** That best score is the only one from this class that counts. Not the class average: the best by everyone else.

**6:04.0** Now do the same for the fifteen classes before and the fifteen after. From each class, keep only its best score.

**6:11.9** Those best scores all land around eighty, and the line through them is the elite curve.

**6:17.1** If one year's test was easier for everyone, its scores are scaled down first, just as every season is converted to the same scoring level.

**6:24.8** The best scores usually sit within about three points of the elite curve. That's one standard deviation.

**6:30.8** So the student's ninety-five is fifteen points above the elite curve: five standard deviations.

**6:37.3** But if the best scores in those years had been around ninety-two, the same ninety-five would be just one standard deviation up.

**6:45.3** Same score, different era. So every season is graded against the best of everyone else in its own era.

## Four rules that keep it fair (6:53.0)

**6:53.8** Four rules keep it fair. First, inflation: when every team scores more, each point is worth a little less, so other seasons are converted into the athlete's own season.

**7:04.8** Second, you're graded against your rivals, not yourself.

**7:11.8** Third, one entry each: an athlete's highest-scoring season.

**7:19.8** And fourth, a season must stand at least one standard deviation clear of every other season on its graph. Stephen Curry's four hundred and two threes score five point six nine, but Ray Allen's 2006 comes within one standard deviation, so Curry is left out.

## How sure is this list? (7:37.4)

**7:38.8** How sure is this list? We ranked every season sixteen ways: different comparison windows, straight or curved trends, with or without the league adjustment.

**7:50.8** Wilt, DiMaggio and Gretzky made the top ten in all sixteen. Rodman and Ichiro, in at least twelve. Those are the five.

**8:00.8** The other five in the top ten by score each rest on one choice of method.

## Five that came close (8:08.8)

**8:11.2** Dazzy Vance's two hundred and sixty-two strikeouts in 1924 rest on the league adjustment. Without it, the bigger totals of Rube Waddell and Walter Johnson win.

**8:21.6** So do Babe Ruth's fifty-four home runs in 1920: without the adjustment, the home-run boom he started catches him.

**8:31.6** Bob Feller's three hundred and forty-eight strikeouts in 1946 slip out of the ten with shorter comparison windows.

**8:41.6** Without the adjustment, Maury Wills's hundred and four steals are overtaken by Lou Brock and Rickey Henderson.

**8:51.6** And Ted Williams's four oh six: without the adjustment, Rogers Hornsby and Bill Terry come within one standard deviation.
