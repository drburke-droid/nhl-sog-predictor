# Narration script: The 10 Biggest Statistical Outliers in Sports History

The film works without a voice: every point is also on screen as a caption. This script is for recording a
voice-over on top. Timecodes are positions in the final cut (`outliers_final.mp4`, 9:46). Each cue lines up with the
caption it reads. About 130 words a minute leaves room for the music; each countdown card is 36 seconds, so keep each
card under about 75 words.

Rules from the production pack, kept throughout:

- Never say "proved", "greatest athlete", "certain" or "once every N seasons".
- Scores are said in round numbers. The exact score is on screen.
- Beamon is one jump. DiMaggio is a streak. Seasons are named by the year they end ("1961–62").
- The league adjustment allows for the league's scoring rate. It does not adjust for minutes, possessions, ice time
  or innings, so never say it does.

## How the list was built (0:23–1:41)

**0:25** Every season's leader in seventy-four categories, across six sports, is compared with the best of everyone
else in the fifteen seasons before and after.

**0:31** Here's an example: the women's eight hundred metres freestyle. Each blue dot is the fastest time that season
by anyone except Katie Ledecky.

**0:39** A curve follows how the event changed over those years.

**0:49** How far the dots usually scatter around that curve becomes the unit of measurement.

**0:59** Ledecky's 2016 swim sits almost five of those units above her era.

**1:07** That ranks twelfth. These are the ten that stood out even further.

**1:15** Three rules. First, team sports are also measured against the league's scoring level. Fifty points a game
means more when teams average a hundred than when they average a hundred and twenty.

**1:21** Second, an athlete's own other seasons are left out, so this measures dominance over rivals.

**1:27** Third, each athlete appears once. A few entries are a single mark or a streak rather than a season total.

**1:33** The score is a statistical distance, not a probability, and the order depends on these choices. More on
that at the end.

## #10 Ichiro Suzuki, 2004 (1:41)

**1:42** Number ten. Ichiro Suzuki, 2004: two hundred and sixty-two hits.

**1:48** The blue dots are the most hits by anyone else in each season, fifteen years either side.

**1:58** His season sits more than five units above that line.

**2:01** It broke George Sisler's record of two fifty-seven, set in 1920. The next best in 2004 was Juan Pierre, with
two twenty-one.

## #9 Babe Ruth, 1920 (2:16)

**2:17** Nine. Babe Ruth, 1920: fifty-four home runs.

**2:24** The chart shows each season's best as a share of what an average team hit, because home runs were rare.

**2:36** His own record from the year before was twenty-nine. The next best in 1920 was George Sisler, with nineteen,
and the average team hit thirty-nine. In raw numbers he's well clear; against how rare home runs were, he's
further out still.

## #8 Dennis Rodman, 1992–93 (2:52)

**2:53** Eight. Dennis Rodman, 1992–93: eighteen point three rebounds a game.

**3:08** The next best that season was a rookie, Shaquille O'Neal, at thirteen point nine.

**3:16** Rodman's eighteen point seven the year before is the bigger raw number, but measured against league-wide
rebounding, 1992–93 stands further out. He led the league in rebounding seven seasons running.

## #7 Stephen Curry, 2015–16 (3:27)

**3:28** Seven. Stephen Curry, 2015–16: four hundred and two three-pointers.

**3:47** The previous record was his own, two eighty-six, from the season before. Second place was his teammate Klay
Thompson, with two seventy-six.

**3:57** Teams have shot more threes every season since, so later totals are measured against a busier league.
Allowing for that raises his score.

## #6 Dazzy Vance, 1924 (4:02)

**4:03** Six. Dazzy Vance, 1924: two hundred and sixty-two strikeouts.

**4:22** No one else in either league had more than a hundred and fifty-eight: Walter Johnson.

**4:27** Strikeouts were scarce, about two point seven per team per game. Without the league adjustment his score is
about three and a half. Scarcity is what lifts it. *(Do not say the score accounts for innings pitched: it doesn't.)*

## #5 Jan Železný, 1996 (4:38)

**4:39** Five. Jan Železný, 1996: ninety-eight point four eight metres with the javelin.

**4:57** The men's javelin was redesigned in 1986, so he's measured only against throws with the new design. The
next best in 1996 was Raymond Hecht, at ninety-two twenty-eight.

**5:08** It broke his own world record from 1993, and thirty years later it still stands.

## #4 Wayne Gretzky, 1985–86 (5:13)

**5:14** Four. Wayne Gretzky, 1985–86: a hundred and sixty-three assists.

**5:33** Second in assists was Mario Lemieux, with ninety-three. Gretzky's assists alone were more than any other
player's points: Lemieux had a hundred and forty-one.

**5:43** Scoring in the mid-eighties was near its peak, and allowing for that brings his score down from about seven
and a half to six and a half.

## #3 Joe DiMaggio, 1941 (5:49)

**5:50** Three. Joe DiMaggio, 1941: a hit in fifty-six straight games. A streak, not a season total.

**6:09** The record before was forty-four, Willie Keeler, in 1897. The longest by anyone else in 1941 was twenty-four.

**6:19** In the eighty-five years since, the closest anyone has come is Pete Rose's forty-four, in 1978.

## #2 Wilt Chamberlain, 1961–62 (6:24)

**6:25** Two. Wilt Chamberlain, 1961–62: fifty point four points a game.

**6:44** The next best was Walt Bellamy, with thirty-one point six, almost nineteen points behind. Chamberlain
averaged forty-eight and a half minutes a game, overtimes included.

**6:54** Teams scored nearly a hundred and nineteen points a game that season. Allowing for that brings his score
down, but only to second. It doesn't account for his minutes or possessions.

## #1 Bob Beamon, 1968 (6:59)

**7:00** And number one. Bob Beamon, Mexico City, 1968: eight point nine zero metres.

**7:19** One jump, not a season total. It beat the world record by fifty-five centimetres.

**7:24** The next best jump that year was eight twenty-eight. Nobody jumped further until Mike Powell, twenty-three
years later.

**7:29** It came at two thousand two hundred and forty metres altitude, with a tailwind of two metres a second: the
legal maximum.

## Why the women's throws aren't on this list (7:35)

**7:36** One more thing: what isn't here. By one measure, the women's shot put and discus records are the most
extreme numbers in the whole dataset.

**7:43** No woman has thrown twenty-two metres in the shot put since 1988. The best this decade is twenty-one
fourteen. The best discus throw since 2000 is more than three metres short of the 1988 record.

**7:54** But in the 1980s, marks like these were routine at the top: eighteen shot puts of twenty-two metres or more,
and fifty discus throws of seventy-two metres or more, by eleven different women.

**8:05** So against their own era, the record holders' best seasons score about two and a half in the shot put and
three in the discus. The top ten starts above five.

**8:15** They were measured like every other event, and they don't make the list. The outlier isn't one athlete.
It's the era.

**8:23** Random out-of-competition drug testing began in athletics in the late 1980s. This film doesn't single
anyone out.

## How sure is this order? (8:32)

**8:33** How sure is this order? We tested sixteen versions of the method: comparison windows from seven to twenty
seasons, straight or curved trends, with or without the league adjustment. Beamon, Chamberlain, DiMaggio and
Gretzky were in the top ten every time. Their order wasn't.

**8:43** And when the comparison seasons are resampled, places five to ten change hands often.

**8:55** Change the question, and the order changes. Count an athlete's own other seasons, and Gretzky falls to
twentieth: his own great seasons become the bar. DiMaggio rises to first.

**9:00** Drop the league adjustment, and Dan Marino's forty-eight touchdown passes and Katie Ledecky's 2016 come
into the top ten.

**9:13** So this isn't a ranking of the greatest athletes. It's a ranking of how far one result stood from everyone
else of its time.

**9:18** Which definition would you choose?
