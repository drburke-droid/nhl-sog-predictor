# Narration script: The 10 Biggest Statistical Outliers in Sports History

The film works without a voice: every point is also on screen as a caption. This script is for recording a
voice-over on top. Timecodes are positions in the final cut (`outliers_final.mp4`, 10:07). Each cue lines up with the
caption it reads. About 130 words a minute leaves room for the music; each countdown card is 36 seconds, so keep each
card under about 75 words.

Rules from the production pack, kept throughout:

- Never say "proved", "greatest athlete", "certain" or "once every N seasons".
- Scores are said in round numbers. The exact score is on screen.
- Beamon is one jump. DiMaggio is a streak. Seasons are named by the year they end ("1961–62").
- The league adjustment allows for the league's scoring rate. It does not adjust for minutes, possessions, ice time
  or innings, so never say it does.

---

## Hook (0:00–0:30)

**0:01** Four thousand nine hundred and forty season-leading performances, across six sports, from 1876 to now.

**0:07** Each one graded against everyone else of its era.

**0:10** Most land near zero: leading your league is normal. Somebody does it every year.

**0:12** Only twelve reach five.

**0:17** And ten broke the curve.

**0:22** *(title: music only)*

**0:25** These are the ten biggest statistical outliers in sports history, counting down from ten.

## #10 Ichiro Suzuki, 2004 (0:29)

**0:30** Number ten. Ichiro Suzuki, 2004: two hundred and sixty-two hits.

**0:36** Each bin holds the most hits by anyone else in a season, fifteen years either side, converted to
2004's hit rate the way old prices are adjusted for inflation.

**0:43** The curve sits at about two hundred and nineteen hits, and one step is about eight. He was forty-three
hits above it: a little over five steps.

**0:49** It broke George Sisler's record of two fifty-seven, set in 1920. The next best in 2004 was Juan Pierre, with
two twenty-one.

## #9 Babe Ruth, 1920 (1:04)

**1:05** Nine. Babe Ruth, 1920: fifty-four home runs.

**1:12** Every other season is converted to 1920's home-run rate. In those terms the curve is about
twenty-three, and one step is between five and six.

**1:24** His own record from the year before was twenty-nine. The next best in 1920 was George Sisler, with nineteen,
and the average team hit thirty-nine. In raw numbers he's well clear; against how rare home runs were, he's
further out still.

## #8 Dennis Rodman, 1992–93 (1:40)

**1:41** Eight. Dennis Rodman, 1992–93: eighteen point three rebounds a game.

**1:56** The next best that season was a rookie, Shaquille O'Neal, at thirteen point nine.

**2:04** Rodman's eighteen point seven the year before is the bigger raw number, but measured against league-wide
rebounding, 1992–93 stands further out. He led the league in rebounding seven seasons running.

## #7 Stephen Curry, 2015–16 (2:15)

**2:16** Seven. Stephen Curry, 2015–16: four hundred and two three-pointers.

**2:35** The previous record was his own, two eighty-six, from the season before. Second place was his teammate Klay
Thompson, with two seventy-six.

**2:45** Teams have shot more threes every season since, so later totals are measured against a busier league.
Allowing for that raises his score.

## #6 Dazzy Vance, 1924 (2:50)

**2:51** Six. Dazzy Vance, 1924: two hundred and sixty-two strikeouts.

**3:10** No one else in either league had more than a hundred and fifty-eight: Walter Johnson.

**3:15** Strikeouts were scarce, about two point seven per team per game. Without the league adjustment his score is
about three and a half. Scarcity is what lifts it. *(Do not say the score accounts for innings pitched: it doesn't.)*

## #5 Jan Železný, 1996 (3:26)

**3:27** Five. Jan Železný, 1996: ninety-eight point four eight metres with the javelin.

**3:45** The men's javelin was redesigned in 1986, so he's measured only against throws with the new design. The
next best in 1996 was Raymond Hecht, at ninety-two twenty-eight.

**3:56** It broke his own world record from 1993, and thirty years later it still stands.

## #4 Wayne Gretzky, 1985–86 (4:01)

**4:02** Four. Wayne Gretzky, 1985–86: a hundred and sixty-three assists.

**4:21** Second in assists was Mario Lemieux, with ninety-three. Gretzky's assists alone were more than any other
player's points: Lemieux had a hundred and forty-one.

**4:31** Scoring in the mid-eighties was near its peak, and allowing for that brings his score down from about seven
and a half to six and a half.

## #3 Joe DiMaggio, 1941 (4:37)

**4:38** Three. Joe DiMaggio, 1941: a hit in fifty-six straight games. A streak, not a season total.

**4:57** The record before was forty-four, Willie Keeler, in 1897. The longest by anyone else in 1941 was twenty-four.

**5:07** In the eighty-five years since, the closest anyone has come is Pete Rose's forty-four, in 1978.

## #2 Wilt Chamberlain, 1961–62 (5:12)

**5:13** Two. Wilt Chamberlain, 1961–62: fifty point four points a game.

**5:32** The next best was Walt Bellamy, with thirty-one point six, almost nineteen points behind. Chamberlain
averaged forty-eight and a half minutes a game, overtimes included.

**5:42** Teams scored nearly a hundred and nineteen points a game that season. Allowing for that brings his score
down, but only to second. It doesn't account for his minutes or possessions.

## #1 Bob Beamon, 1968 (5:47)

**5:48** And number one. Bob Beamon, Mexico City, 1968: eight point nine zero metres.

**6:07** One jump, not a season total. It beat the world record by fifty-five centimetres.

**6:12** The next best jump that year was eight twenty-eight. Nobody jumped further until Mike Powell, twenty-three
years later.

**6:17** It came at two thousand two hundred and forty metres altitude, with a tailwind of two metres a second: the
legal maximum.

## How the score works: grading on a curve (6:23–7:07)

**6:25** So how does the score work? Picture a class test. Most of the class scores around seventy, give or take
about five points.

**6:33** Call that usual give-or-take one step.

**6:39** A student who scores ninety-five is twenty-five points above the class average. That's five steps, so on
our scale it's a score of five.

**6:50** Now put the same ninety-five in a class that averages ninety. It's one step above: a score of one.

**6:59** Same number, different class. Every result here is graded against its own era: everyone else in the fifteen seasons either side.

## Three rules that keep it fair (7:07–7:46)

**7:08** Three rules keep it fair. First, inflation: when every team scores more, each point is worth less. In team sports, others' numbers are converted to the athlete's season.

**7:19** Second, you're graded against rivals, not yourself. Otherwise Gretzky would be graded against Gretzky.

**7:27** Third, one entry each: the athlete's highest-scoring season, or for a few, a single jump or streak.

**7:36** The score measures distance from the class, not talent, and it isn't a probability.

## Why the women's throws aren't on this list (7:46)

**7:47** What isn't here: by one measure, the women's shot put and discus records are the most extreme in the dataset.

**7:54** No woman has thrown twenty-two metres in the shot put since 1988. Since 2000, no discus throw has come within three metres of the record.

**8:05** But in the 1980s, marks like these were routine: eighteen shot puts of at least twenty-two metres, and fifty discus throws of at least seventy-two, by eleven women.

**8:16** So against their own era, the record holders' best seasons score about two and a half in the shot put and
three in the discus. The top ten starts above five.

**8:26** They were measured like every other event, and they don't make the list. The outlier isn't one athlete.
It's the era.

**8:34** Random out-of-competition drug testing began in athletics in the late 1980s. This film doesn't single
anyone out.

## How sure is this order? (8:43)

**8:44** How sure is this order? Across sixteen versions of the method, Beamon, Chamberlain, DiMaggio and Gretzky made the top ten every time.

**8:54** Their order shifted. And when the comparison seasons are resampled, places five to ten change hands often.

**9:06** Count their own seasons too, and Gretzky falls to twentieth. DiMaggio rises to first.

**9:11** Drop the league adjustment, and Dan Marino's forty-eight touchdown passes and Katie Ledecky's 2016 come
into the top ten.

**9:24** So this isn't a ranking of the greatest athletes.

**9:29** It ranks how far one result stood from everyone else of its time. Which definition would you choose?
