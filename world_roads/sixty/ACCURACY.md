# Why 60? Why 24? Accuracy notes

These notes cover what the film claims, where each claim comes from, what the pictures simplify, and which
ideas are only theories. Every caption was checked against the sources listed in section 2. Where scholars
disagree, or the evidence is thin, the caption says so ("one theory", "one ancient text", "about").

## 1. What is drawn, and what is simplified

| On screen | What it is |

## 2. Claims and sources

### 2.1 Counting in tens; base 60 in Mesopotamia
- The earliest written accounts, proto-cuneiform tablets from Uruk (c. 3300–3000 BCE), already count discrete
  objects in a system that steps by 10s and 6s (1, 10, 60, 600, 3600).
- Full place-value notation appears in Ur III scratch calculations (c. 2100–2000 BCE). It was the standard
  working notation of Old Babylonian scribes (c. 2000–1600 BCE). Hence the caption "About 4,000 years ago".
- Sources: Wikipedia, "Proto-cuneiform", citing Englund, Nissen and Damerow. J. Friberg, *Cuneiform Digital
  Library Journal* 2005:2.

### 2.2 Two signs, and no zero
- Just two signs (1 and 10), with no zero and no "sexagesimal point". Readers worked out the scale from
  context.
- Later scribes added a placeholder for empty places in the middle of a number: a Kish tablet c. 700 BCE,
  and two slanted wedges by the Seleucid era. It was never used at the end of a number or on its own.
- The caption "There was no zero yet" refers to the Old Babylonian period shown.
- Sources: Wikipedia, "Babylonian cuneiform numerals" (citing S. Chrisomalis, *Numerical Notation*, CUP 2010,
  pp. 247–248). J. J. O'Connor and E. F. Robertson, "Babylonian numerals", MacTutor (2000).

### 2.3 YBC 7289
- 1;24,51,10 = 1 + 24/60 + 51/60² + 10/60³ = 1.414 212 96…; √2 = 1.414 213 56…. The difference is
  5.99 × 10⁻⁷, so the value is right to within six ten-millionths (relative error 4.2 × 10⁻⁷).
- The tablet is Old Babylonian (c. 1800–1600 BCE), so the captions say "about 1700 BC" and the thumbnail
  says "3,700-year-old". 42;25,35 is exactly 30 × 1;24,51,10, the diagonal of a square of side 30.
- Source: D. Fowler and E. Robson, "Square root approximations in Old Babylonian mathematics: YBC 7289 in
  context", *Historia Mathematica* 25 (1998) 366–378.

### 2.4 Why 60
- Nobody knows why 60 was chosen. Its 12 divisors (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30, 60) made
  division easy. Scribes divided by multiplying with tabulated reciprocals, which terminate only for "regular"
  numbers (factors 2, 3 and 5). That explains why 60 was convenient, not why it was chosen.
- The finger-bone story (the thumb counts 12 segments, the other hand's 5 fingers count dozens) is labelled
  "one theory", and the next caption says "there's no ancient evidence for it". The counting method is real
  and still used across West and South Asia; the link to Mesopotamia is a modern conjecture, spread by popular
  histories.
- MacTutor lists the competing theories (weights and measures, a 360-day year, two peoples merging) and judges
  that none is convincing. Specialists tie base 60 to early accounting and measurement systems.
- Sources: MacTutor, "Babylonian numerals". M. A. Powell, "The origin of the sexagesimal system", *Visible
  Language* 6 (1972) 5–18.

### 2.5 The Babylonian day: 12 bēru, 360 UŠ
- Babylonian astronomers split the full day (day plus night) into 12 bēru of 30 UŠ each: 360 UŠ. One UŠ is
  4 of our minutes, about the time the sky takes to turn 1°.
- "Double hour" is a modern gloss: both units began as measures of distance. These were equal fractions of
  the whole day, not seasonal hours. The Babylonian day began at sunset; the diagram doesn't show where it
  begins.
- Attested in MUL.APIN (earliest copy 686 BCE), the Astronomical Diaries (652–60 BCE) and later
  astronomical texts. That is why the caption says "Babylonian astronomers" rather than "Sumerians".
- Sources: J. M. Steele, "Shadow-length schemes in Babylonian astronomy", *SCIAMVS* 14 (2013) 3–39.
  M. Ossendrijver, Rostovtzeff Lectures 2018, lecture 2.

### 2.6 The zodiac and the 360° circle
- The zodiac of 12 signs of 30° was invented around the end of the 5th century BCE, so the caption says
  "Later". Greek astronomy took the 360° circle and sexagesimal arithmetic from Babylon; the earliest surviving
  Greek text with 360 degrees is Hypsicles (2nd century BCE).
- Why 360 is unknown. The film doesn't claim it came from a 360-day year.
- Sources: J. M. Steele, "Geminos and Babylonian astronomy" (2020), citing J. P. Britton, *Archive for History
  of Exact Sciences* 64 (2010) 617–663. Kwan, "Hipparchus of Nicaea", *Biographical Encyclopedia of
  Astronomers* (Springer 2007).

### 2.7 Egypt: decans, 12 night hours, and 12 day hours
- 36 decans, each marking a ten-day week: 36 × 10 = 360, plus 5 extra (epagomenal) days makes the 365-day
  civil year.
- Star tables ("diagonal star clocks"), most from about 2100 BCE and painted mainly on coffin lids, split the
  night into 12 parts, each marked by a decan.
- Why 12 follows Parker: the tables measured only the time of full darkness, when about 12 decans rose. With
  five-day weeks the count would have been about 26. The captions say "About 12 of them rose one after another
  in the full dark of night". The film does not use the popular "midsummer, near Sirius's rising" version,
  which the fact-check could not trace to Neugebauer and Parker.
- These star-table "hours" were roughly 40 minutes and irregular. Symons suggests the tables worked more like
  almanacs than clocks.
- The day: "10 hours of sunlight plus an hour of twilight at each end" is Parker's reading of the shadow-clock
  text in the cenotaph of Seti I at Abydos (c. 1300 BCE). The text is part of the "Book of Nut" and copies
  older material, and its interpretation is still discussed. So the caption says "one ancient text".
  The older shadow clock of Thutmose III (Berlin ÄM 19744) is read by Parker as 12 hours from sunrise to
  sunset; the film doesn't tie "10 + 2" to it.
- These were seasonal hours: a twelfth of the daylight, or of the night.
- In Parker's summary, Egypt's contribution "did not go beyond the concept of twenty-four… hours to the day";
  the minutes and seconds came from Babylonian base-60 astronomy through the Greeks. That is the film's thesis
  in one sentence.
- Sources: R. A. Parker, "Egyptian astronomy, astrology, and calendrical reckoning", *Dictionary of Scientific
  Biography* (1978). S. Symons and E. Tasker, "Decoding the star charts of ancient Egypt", *Scientific
  American*, October 2015. McMaster Ancient Egyptian sundials database, entries "SL Berlin ÄM 19744" and
  "SL Osireion".

### 2.8 Greek astronomy: equal hours, minutes, seconds
- Hipparchus's observations date from 147–127 BCE, hence "in the 2nd century BC". His *Commentary on Aratus*
  groups stars by the 24 equal (equinoctial) hours. Seasonal hours stayed in everyday use until mechanical
  clocks.
- Ptolemy and other astronomers wrote fractions in sixtieths and sixtieths of sixtieths. In medieval Latin
  these became *pars minuta prima* ("first small part"), the minute, and *pars minuta secunda* ("second small
  part"), the second.
- Al-Bīrūnī's *Chronology of Ancient Nations* (c. AD 1000) tabulates times in days, hours, minutes, seconds,
  thirds and fourths, and defines thirds as "the 60th parts of a second". This was checked in Sachau's 1879
  translation.
- Sources: M. Lombardi (NIST), "Why is a minute divided into 60 seconds, an hour into 60 minutes, yet there
  are only 24 hours in a day?", *Scientific American*, 5 March 2007. Al-Bīrūnī, trans. E. Sachau (1879).
  Wikipedia, "Minute" and "Second".

### 2.9 Clocks
- Public striking clocks spread through European towns from about 1300: Dohrn-van Rossum counts nearly 500
  first references between 1300 and 1450. Most had only an hour hand.
- Minute hands existed by the late 1500s but were rare. They became the rule around 1680–90, after
  Christiaan Huygens's pendulum clock (invented 25 December 1656, patented 1657). It improved a good clock's
  error from about 15 minutes a day to about 15 seconds.
- Second hands appeared in a few clocks from c. 1560–1580. They became usual with the anchor escapement and
  the seconds pendulum, from c. 1670. Who invented the anchor escapement is disputed, so the film names no one.
- Sources: Wikipedia, "Pendulum clock" and "Anchor escapement" (citing Andrewes 1994; Milham 1945; D. Landes,
  *Revolution in Time*, 1983). G. Dohrn-van Rossum, *History of the Hour* (1996).

### 2.10 French decimal time
- Decreed 5 October 1793 (14 Vendémiaire, Year II, art. 11) and restated on 24 November 1793. Use in public
  records became compulsory on 22 September 1794.
- Suspended by the law of 18 Germinal, Year III (7 April 1795), the same law that set up the metric weights
  and measures.
- Conversions: 86,400 s ÷ 10 = 8,640 s = 2 h 24 min; ÷ 100 = 86.4 s; ÷ 100 = 0.864 s. Noon is half a day:
  5 decimal hours.
- Sources: Wikipedia, "Decimal time" (decree text). French Wikipedia, "Temps décimal en France" (citing Gamez
  2020, pp. 57–59).

### 2.11 Other ways to cut a day
- **China.** The day had 100 kè by the first millennium BC, with brief trials of 120, 96 and 108. The 12
  double hours (shí) ran alongside, at 8⅓ kè each. Jesuit-assisted calendar reform in the 1600s (Sôma et al.
  date the 96-kè system to 1628; it was made official in the Shixian calendar, 1645) set 96 kè, so a kè became
  15 minutes. 刻 still means a quarter of an hour.
- **India.** Sūrya Siddhānta 1.11: six breaths make a vināḍī, sixty vināḍī a nāḍī, and sixty nāḍī a sidereal
  day. So a nāḍī (ghaṭikā) is 24 minutes and a vināḍī (vighaṭikā, pala) is 24 seconds.
- Sources: M. Sôma, K. Kawabata and K. Tanikawa, "Units of time in ancient China and Japan", *PASJ* 56 (2004)
  887–904. Wikipedia, "Decimal time" (citing Needham et al. 1986 and Yabuuti 1963). E. Burgess (trans.),
  *Sūrya Siddhānta* (1860).

### 2.12 The SI second and the scale of time
- The 13th CGPM (1967) defined the second as 9,192,631,770 periods of the radiation of the caesium-133
  ground-state hyperfine transition. Since 2019 the definition fixes that frequency instead; the duration is
  the same.
- The minute, hour and day are "non-SI units accepted for use with the SI" (SI Brochure, Table 8).
- Earth turns 360° relative to the Sun in 24 hours, 15° an hour. Nominal time zones are 15° wide, and real
  ones follow borders.
- Source: BIPM, *The International System of Units*, 9th edition (2019).

## 3. Computed numbers

- **YBC 7289.** See 2.3.
- **Seasonal hours at Thebes (Luxor, 25.70° N).** Day length comes from the sunrise equation, with the Sun's
  centre 0.833° below the horizon (refraction plus the Sun's radius) and a declination of ±23.44°.
  - Midsummer: 13.74 h of daylight. A day hour is 68.7 min (shown as 69) and a night hour 51.3 min (shown as
    51).
  - Midwinter: 10.53 h of daylight. A day hour is 52.7 min (53) and a night hour 67.3 min (67).
  - These use modern definitions of sunrise and sunset. An Egyptian count that included twilight hours would
    differ.
- **The ring of 60.** It splits into 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20 or 30 equal groups. The ring of 10 splits
  into 2 or 5; split into 3, it leaves 1 over.

## 4. Sound

The score is synthesized: a plucked lyre (additive partials), a pad, a clock tick and a small bell. Its tempo
is exactly 60 beats per minute. Every section starts on a whole second (whole-second durations, 1 s
crossfades), so each tick lands with the on-screen second hands of the ordinary clocks. The decimal clock's
second hand ticks every 0.864 s and deliberately does not line up. A bell rings at each full minute of the
film. Loudness is −16 LUFS.
