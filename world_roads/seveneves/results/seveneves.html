Testing a novel with an N-body simulation

Seveneves Simulator

In Neal Stephenson's Seveneves the Moon breaks into seven pieces. They grind each other to rubble, and two years later the "Hard Rain" begins: thousands of years of falling Moon that sterilise the Earth's surface. We simulated it: real gravity, real collision physics, and real steam tables.

The setup

Seven pieces, the Sun, the Earth, and the debris

The Moon is cut into seven uneven pieces, from 6% to 19% of its mass. They get just enough energy to escape each other's gravity, plus a leftover drift speed: the main dial. The Sun, the Earth and every fragment bigger than 80 km are integrated together with full gravity. When two fragments touch, collision scaling laws for planet-sized bodies decide whether they merge, chip or shatter. Every parcel of debris, from the breakup itself and from those collisions, is then followed until it hits the Earth, hits a fragment, or leaves Earth orbit. The heat of whatever hits the Earth goes into a climate model that has to boil the oceans to get hot.

The dial that matters

How much dust did the breakup make?

The novel never says how much of the Moon was turned straight into small debris by whatever broke it. It is the setting that matters most for the Earth. Because debris parcels are followed as test particles, we can re-weight them without re-running anything.

What this leaves out

Limits

  • Debris parcels don't collide with each other. Grinding into dust could spread their orbits (more rain) or flatten them into a ring (less).
  • Pieces thrown out of Earth orbit go round the Sun near the Earth's own orbit. Over 10,000 to a million years many would come back. They are not followed.
  • The climate is one global column: no geography, clouds, impact winter or melting crust. Bare land under that sky would be far hotter than the global average.
  • Whatever broke the Moon is not modelled; only its results are set by the dials.

Physics: REBOUND IAS15 (Rein & Spiegel 2015); collisions, Leinhardt & Stewart 2012 and Kokubo & Genda 2010; crater ejecta, Housen & Holsapple 2011; runaway-greenhouse limit, Goldblatt et al. 2013; steam, IAPWS-IF97.
