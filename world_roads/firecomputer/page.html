After the signal fires

The Fire Computer

A chain of signal fires can carry a message from mountain to mountain. Give each fire a keeper and a rule, and a field of them can calculate. A lit fire is a 1 and a dark fire is a 0. Every keeper watches two fires and carries the same card.

Every keeper's card
Keep your fire lit, unless both of the fires you watch are lit.
dark, dark→lit
dark, lit→lit
lit, dark→lit
lit, lit→dark
The memory keeper's card (Act III)
When the drum beats, make your fire match the one you watch. Between beats, leave it alone.
Every fire has a lid, so a keeper can show or hide its light as often as needed.

Engineers call the first card NAND ("not and"). Every other rule, and every circuit on this page, is built from it. The only difference between keepers is which two fires each one watches. Each keeper takes about 2 seconds to see a change, check the card and move the lid.

Act I · 7 fires

1 + 1, with five keepers

Two fires on top hold the numbers being added, A and B. Five keepers below them add the numbers. The fire on the bottom right shows the ones place, and the fire on the bottom left shows the twos place. Tap A or B to change it, and watch the change pass down one keeper at a time.

The first keeper (the middle fire) goes dark only when A and B are both lit. The two keepers under it each compare that fire with A or with B, and the ones fire combines them. Together those four NAND fires make "lit if exactly one of A and B is lit". The twos fire flips the first keeper's fire: it is lit only when both A and B are. That is binary addition: 1 + 1 = 10, one two and no ones.

Act II · 40 fires and three ridges

Sightlines are wires

Two hill villages each count their sacks of grain and send the count down a ridge as 4 lidded fires: the 8s, 4s, 2s and 1s. Each relay copies the four fires it sees, like the signal-fire chains, but with lids instead of new bonfires. In the valley, 32 keepers add the two counts with four copies of the Act I trick, each passing its carry to the next. The total climbs the far ridge to the mill.

Act III · 2,110 fires and a chain to Paris

A message the sky can't read

In the analog-backup story, an AI holds every phone, cable and satellite, and the warning goes out by bonfire. A satellite can see every bonfire, though, and so can the AI. To keep a message private you need a real cipher. Real ciphers are far too long to work by hand:

So Bletchley Park, where Colossus broke codes in the war, builds one out of fire. The field runs PRESENT, a published lightweight cipher (ISO/IEC 29192-2) with a 128-bit key that no computer could guess. It sits under a roof so the sky can't see it. It scrambles 8 letters at a time in 31 rounds, one round per drum beat, and the scrambled digits go out on the open beacon chain. Paris builds an identical field with the same key, and the same steps turn the scramble back into the message.

Out along the beacon chain

The scrambled block goes out as hex digits, 4 bits each, using the beacon chain Video 2 lit from Bletchley Park to Paris. Each station has 4 lidded fires for the bits and a fifth that is lit while a digit is showing. Every keeper can see the digits, and so can the satellites, but without the key they mean nothing.

How big it gets

Every circuit, counted fire by fire

Every row was built from the same NAND card (plus memory keepers for the cipher), then run keeper by keeper and checked against the right answers. "Keepers deep" is the longest chain of keepers a change has to pass through, one after another.

Fire against pencil

When the field is worth lighting

For everyday arithmetic, the field loses. One person multiplies 2,400 × 365 with a pencil in about a minute, while the multiplier field needs 2,640 keepers and about five minutes. The cipher is different because it is long, not because it is hard. It is thousands of tiny steps, and a single slip ruins the whole block, which is the kind of job a field of rule-followers does well. None of the keepers needs to know any arithmetic, or even what the field is for.

That is also the point of the whole exercise. Computers aren't smart. They are enormous numbers of very simple rule-followers. A phone has billions of them, each switching billions of times a second. In The Three-Body Problem, an emperor's army becomes a computer, each soldier raising or lowering a flag by a rule. The fire field is the same idea, at a size and speed you can watch.

Built and checked by world_roads/firecomputer/build.py: every circuit is a list of who watches whom, run one keeper step (2 s) at a time. The adders are checked on every input or 200 random sums, the multipliers on 200 random products, and the cipher on the four published PRESENT-128 test vectors plus 20 random blocks against plain software PRESENT. Assumed timings: 2 s per field keeper; 10 s per mountain hop with lidded fires; 30 s per hex digit on the chain (shown for 20 s, dark for 10 s). The beacon chain is Video 2's terrain-checked route. The cipher runs in counter mode: the field scrambles the block number 1588 × 65,536 + n with the key and adds the result to the message, so the same field both scrambles and unscrambles.
