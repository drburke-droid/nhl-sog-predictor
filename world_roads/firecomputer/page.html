After the signal fires

The Fire Computer

A chain of signal fires can carry one piece of news across a continent. This page shows, one small step at a time, how a field of fires and the people who tend them can calculate: first 1 + 1, then real sums, and at the end a secret code that would take a person most of a day to work by hand.

No maths needed to start. Each of the twelve levels adds one idea and has something to try. The bar beside each title shows how steep that level is.

Part one

Fires as numbers

1. One fire, one answer

A signal fire answers one yes-or-no question. Is the fleet coming? Lit means yes and dark means no. Nothing in between.

Computers use the same idea. Write a lit fire as 1 and a dark fire as 0. One fire holds one of these answers, and computer people call it a bit.

Try it: tap the fire

2. Fires that count

One fire can only say yes or no. A row of fires can say a number, if each fire is worth something different. Give the fires the values 8, 4, 2 and 1, and add up the lit ones.

It works like a set of weights on a market scale. With one 8 kg, one 4 kg, one 2 kg and one 1 kg weight, you can weigh out any whole amount from 0 to 15 kg: 11 kg is 8 + 2 + 1. This way of writing numbers is called binary, and 11 is written 1011.

Try it: make 13. Then make 15, the most four fires can show.

Each extra fire doubles how far the row can count. 8 fires reach 255, 16 reach 65,535, and 64 fires can hold 8 letters of text.

Part two

Keepers and their cards

3. The relay keeper is a wire

Every fire has a keeper, and every keeper carries a card with one rule. The simplest card is the one from the signal-fire chains: copy the fire you watch.

Each fire also has a lid, like a brazier with a cover, so a keeper can hide the light again as easily as show it. A keeper takes about 2 seconds to notice a change, check the card and move the lid. A line of copying keepers carries a 1 or a 0 from one place to another, which is exactly what a wire does in an electronic computer.

The relay card
Make your fire match the one you watch.
Try it: tap the first fire and watch the change travel

4. Keepers that decide

A relay keeper only passes news on. A keeper becomes useful when the card makes a decision. Here are three cards. The first watches one fire, the other two watch two fires, A and B.

NOT
Light yours if the fire you watch is dark.
AND
Light yours only if both fires you watch are lit.
OR
Light yours if either fire you watch is lit.
Cardthen tap A or B

A list like this, every possible case and its answer, is called a truth table.

5. One card is enough

Here is a surprise that makes the whole computer possible. You don't need a different card for every job. One card, carried by every keeper, can do all of them. It is AND with the answer flipped, so engineers call it NAND ("not and").

Below, the same two fires A and B feed three little groups of NAND keepers. A keeper who watches the same fire twice acts as NOT. Two keepers make AND, and three make OR. The only difference between the groups is who watches whom.

Every keeper's card from here on
Keep your fire lit, unless both of the fires you watch are lit.
Try it: tap A or B. All three groups use only the NAND card.

From here on, every circuit on this page uses the same NAND card. Building a computer is mostly a matter of deciding which two fires each keeper watches.

Part three

Arithmetic

6. 1 + 1, with five keepers

Adding two single fires has only four cases: 0 + 0 = 0, 0 + 1 = 1, 1 + 0 = 1, and 1 + 1 = 2. In binary, 2 is written 10 (one 2 and no 1s), so the answer needs two fires: a ones fire and a twos fire.

Look at the pattern. The ones fire is lit when exactly one of A and B is lit. The twos fire is lit only when both are. Five NAND keepers do both jobs, sharing the middle keeper.

Try it: tap A or B and count the keeper steps

7. Carrying the one

To add bigger numbers, do what you learned at school: add one column at a time, and carry when a column overflows. Binary works the same way, except a column overflows at 2 instead of 10.

Every column after the first has three fires to add: one from each number, plus the carry from the column to its right. Nine NAND keepers handle one column. They do Level 6's trick twice: first A + B, then that answer plus the carry.

Try it: one column. Tap A, B or the carry coming in.

Line up four of these columns, each passing its carry to the next, and the field can add any two numbers up to 15.

8. Sightlines are wires

Now put the adding field in a valley. Two hill villages each count their sacks of grain and send the count down a ridge as 4 lidded fires (the 8s, 4s, 2s and 1s). Each station copies the four fires it sees, like a signal-fire chain carrying four bits side by side. A mountain hop is slower than a field keeper, so take it as 10 seconds.

In the valley, 32 keepers add the counts: one Level 6 group for the first column and three Level 7 columns. The total climbs the far ridge to the mill as 5 fires.

Part four

Memory

9. The drum

So far the field only answers what it is shown. A computer also has to remember, so it can work in steps: do something, keep the result, then do the next thing to it. That needs a second kind of keeper with a second card.

Why a drum? While the field works, fires flicker as changes ripple through. Memory keepers must not copy halfway through, or they would copy a wrong answer. So they wait for the drum, which beats only after the field has had time to settle. Then they all copy at the same moment, like rowers pulling on the beat. Electronic computers do the same thing with a clock. When a phone's chip is advertised at "3 GHz", that means its drum beats 3 billion times a second.

The memory keeper's card
When the drum beats, make your fire match the one you watch. Between beats, leave it alone.

Here the three memory fires on top hold a number. The keepers below them work out that number plus one (Level 6's trick again). On every beat, the memory keepers copy the answer, so the field counts.

Keepers that calculate, plus memory, plus a drum: that is a complete computer. Everything after this is the same three ingredients, just more of them.

Part five

Something worth the trouble

10. Scrambling a message

Back to the signal-fire story. An AI has taken every phone, cable and satellite, and the warning goes out by bonfire. But a satellite can see every bonfire, and so can the AI. Anything we send across the hills, it reads. We need a secret code, a cipher, and the two ends need to share a secret pattern, the key, agreed in person beforehand.

A good cipher uses three simple moves, repeated many times.

Move 1: mix in the key

Wherever the key fire is lit, flip the message fire. Where the key is dark, leave it alone. Do it a second time with the same key and every flip is undone. That is how the other end gets the message back.

Move 2: swap by the table

Mixing alone is too easy to undo by guesswork. So every group of 4 fires is swapped for a different pattern, using a fixed table everyone knows. The table is designed so that changing one fire going in changes about half the fires coming out. In the field, each table takes 72 NAND keepers.

Move 3: shuffle

Finally, the 64 fires of a block change places, so each table's output feeds four different tables next time. In the field this move costs no fires at all: each memory keeper simply watches a different fire.

11. The field at Bletchley Park

So Bletchley Park, where Colossus broke codes in the war, builds a cipher field out of fire, under a roof so the sky can't see it. It has 2,110 fires: 64 memory fires for the block, 128 for the key, 5 that count the rounds, 64 for the message, and 1,849 NAND keepers doing the three moves. One drum beat is one round.

One more trick keeps it simple. The field doesn't scramble the message directly. It scrambles a block number, using the key, into 64 fires that look random: a mask. Then it mixes the mask into the message with Move 1. Paris builds the same field with the same key, makes the same mask, and mixes it in again, which flips every fire back.

12. Out along the beacon chain

The scrambled block goes out on the open beacon chain from the Video 2 route, Bletchley Park to Paris: 10 hops and 476 km, including one jump across the Channel. Each station shows one hex digit at a time (4 bits, written 0–9 and a–f) with 4 lidded fires, plus a fifth that is lit while a digit is showing. Every keeper on the way can see the digits, and so can the satellites, but without the key they mean nothing.

Afterwards

How big it gets

Every row was built from the same NAND card (plus memory keepers where there's a drum), then run keeper by keeper and checked against the right answers. "Keepers deep" is the longest chain of keepers a change has to pass through, one after another.

When the field is worth lighting

For everyday arithmetic, the field loses. One person multiplies 2,400 × 365 with a pencil in about a minute, while the multiplier field needs 2,640 keepers and about five minutes. The cipher is different because it is long, not because it is hard. It is thousands of tiny steps where a single slip ruins the whole block. That is the kind of job a field of rule-followers does well, and none of them needs to know any arithmetic, or even what the field is for.

That is also the point of the whole exercise. Computers aren't smart. They are enormous numbers of very simple rule-followers. A phone has billions of them, each switching billions of times a second. In The Three-Body Problem, an emperor's army becomes a computer, each soldier raising or lowering a flag by a rule. The fire field is the same idea, at a size and speed you can watch.
