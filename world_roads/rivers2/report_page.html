Rivers and π, part two

Rivers Beyond π

We went back to the rivers with far more data: every reach of the US national river network and every river line mapped in OpenStreetMap. The π claim fails again, at every size. Three other patterns came through clearly, and one of them repeats a little-known 2017 discovery almost to the degree.

US river reaches US rivers, source to mouth km of mapped river worldwide US river junctions
  1. Rivers branch wider where the climate is wetter. Junction angles rise from about 44° in deserts to about 72° in humid country, as Seybold, Rothman and Kirchner found in 2017.
  2. No single ratio fits rivers, because rivers are slightly fractal. The length-to-straight-line ratio grows with size, from 1.1 for creeks to about 2 for great rivers. It reaches π for almost none.
  3. Hack’s 1957 law holds across two million reaches, with an exponent of 0.58 that eases toward 0.5 in big basins.
  4. The Earth’s spin barely shapes rivers. Baer’s 1860 law, which Einstein explained in his meander paper, shows no sign in nine million kilometres of river. At most a faint trace appears in the biggest rivers of Russia, where Baer did his fieldwork.
Finding 1 · confirms Seybold et al. 2017

Wet climates make wider river junctions

Where two streams meet, the angle between them is a record of climate. In a dry landscape, water runs off the surface in sheets and carves narrow, steep branches. In a wet one, springs fed by groundwater eat into the ground and the branches spread. Theory for spring-fed networks predicts 72°, or 2π/5 (Devauchelle et al. 2012). Seybold, Rothman and Kirchner measured junctions across the United States in 2017 and reported about 45° in arid regions and 72° in humid ones.

We measured every confluence of two natural channels in the lower 48 states, using the straight line from the junction to a point 1 km up each branch. The climate at each junction is its aridity index: yearly precipitation divided by the water the air could evaporate (Thornthwaite’s method, from NHDPlus’s monthly PRISM climate).

The trend holds within flat, middling and steep ground, and for junctions of two headwater streams alone. The distance up each branch shifts every angle, since channels bend as they approach a junction, but not the gap between dry and wet. Steeper ground also narrows junctions, as Howard (1971) predicted.

Finding 2 · the π claim, at every size

No single ratio fits rivers

The story goes that a river’s length divided by the straight line from source to mouth averages π. We now have that ratio for US rivers, every main stem in the national network from a 1 km creek to the Missouri, and for the world’s river systems longer than 100 km.

The idea traces to Hans-Henrik Stølum’s 1996 model, in which meandering rivers organise themselves around a sinuosity of about π. But Stølum’s ratio was channel length over valley length along a meandering stretch, not the straight line from source to mouth; the popular version stretched it.

Measured from source to mouth, the ratio is not a constant at all. It grows with the size of the river, from about 1.1 for creeks to about 2 for the great rivers, and it levels off there. Only of US rivers land within 0.2 of π. The growth is what you expect if rivers are slightly fractal: across US rivers, length rises roughly as the straight-line distance to the power 1.07. Mandelbrot (1982) argued from Hack’s law that rivers are fractal, with an exponent a little above 1. A fractal has no single ratio, so π could only ever be true of rivers of one particular size.

Finding 3 · Hack’s law

Big basins grow longer, at a slowing rate

In 1957 John Hack measured streams in Virginia and Maryland and found that a river’s length grows as the 0.6 power of the area it drains. Double the basin and the river gets about 50% longer, not twice as long, so big basins are fatter than small ones. Later work found the exponent slips toward 0.5 for the largest basins (Mueller 1972). Here it is for reaches of the US network.

Finding 4 · Baer’s law and Einstein

The Earth’s spin barely bends rivers

In 1860 Karl Ernst von Baer, studying Russian rivers, proposed that the Earth’s rotation pushes a river’s water toward its right bank in the northern hemisphere, so right banks erode more. Einstein’s 1926 paper on meanders, the one with the tea leaves, is titled after this law and argues it should be real.

If right banks erode more, left-hand bends grow a little more, because a bend’s outer bank is the one that wears away. So a slightly larger share of a northern river’s length should curve left, and of a southern river’s length should curve right. We measured the share curving left along every mapped river line, smoothed at the scale of bends.

Share of river length curving left, every OpenStreetMap river line

Across nine million kilometres, the share is 50.02% in both hemispheres. Every well-sampled band of latitude sits within about a tenth of a percent of 50%; only the sparse far south wanders further, and its interval includes 50%. Left and right bends are equally tight, too. Rotation should matter most for large, slow rivers with wide bends, so we ran the test again on the main stems of the 1,000 longest rivers.

Bends smoothed at 5 km; bars are 95% intervals from resampling rivers
Show the numbers

Here a trace appears. In big northern rivers, 50.4% of curving length bends left, and 55% of the rivers curve left more than right. The excess is larger at high latitudes, where the rotational force is stronger. But almost all of it comes from Russia and Siberia, the rivers Baer studied, where permafrost and lopsided valleys offer other explanations. Without Russia, the northern figure falls inside chance. The southern rivers lean the predicted way, but within chance. A test on all 1,525 named rivers of the 7,311 systems, with a larger southern sample, finds nothing significant in either hemisphere. Our verdict: rotation is at most a whisper in the shape of rivers.

Also in the data

  • No direction dominates. Of the world’s 7,311 systems over 100 km, run north, east, south and west, measured from source to mouth. The Nile flowing north is not unusual.
  • The world’s big rivers settle near 2. Every size class of the 7,311 systems has a median length-to-straight-line ratio between 1.88 and 2.13. About 4% fall within 0.2 of π.

How this was done

  • US network: NHDPlus Version 2 (US EPA and USGS, 1:100,000), all 21 processing units of the lower 48: 2.69 million flowlines, their stream order, drainage area, slope and network links, and each catchment’s monthly precipitation and temperature (PRISM, 1971–2000).
  • Junction angles: every node where two natural channels (not canals, pipes or lake paths) join into one, with matching tributary end points and no braiding. Angle between straight lines from the junction to points 250 m, 500 m and 1 km up each branch. Intervals come from resampling whole 1° cells, since nearby junctions are not independent.
  • Aridity: annual precipitation over Thornthwaite (1948) potential evapotranspiration, from each catchment’s twelve monthly temperatures and its latitude. Seybold et al. used a different index (Zomer’s global aridity map), so the axis matches theirs only roughly.
  • Hack’s law: for each reach, the longest channel upstream of its outlet, against the area draining to it. Basins under 1 km² are left out, because at this map scale the channels stop short of the divide, and so are basins over about 18,000 km², which span several processing units.
  • Main stems: NHDPlus’s level paths, each a river from its source to where it ends, at a larger river or the sea. Length is the sum of its reaches; the straight line runs from first point to last.
  • World rivers: every OpenStreetMap waterway=river line, via Overture Maps (the data behind our π video). Baer test: each line at least 2 km long, resampled every 25 m, with heading smoothed at 250 m and 1 km; OSM draws waterways downstream. The 1,000 longest main stems are rebuilt from the π project’s river systems and smoothed at 1 to 10 km.
  • Not reached: published river lengths (Wikidata, Wikipedia), the global HydroSHEDS network, and journal websites are blocked from this computer. The references below are from memory, and their page numbers are unchecked.
