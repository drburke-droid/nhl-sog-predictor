## How to generate it

- Generate **one audio file per scene**, named as below (for example `01_cold_open.mp3`), and send them to me. I
  cut the visuals to each file's exact length, so retakes of single scenes are easy.
- Keep the same voice and settings for every scene. A good start: Multilingual v2 (or the newest model),
  stability about 0.45-0.55, similarity about 0.75, style low (0-0.2), speaker boost on.
- If a word comes out wrong, respell it the way it should sound, in that scene only. One to check: "Magie". I
  haven't confirmed how Lizzie Magie said her name, so listen to how a documentary or interview says it and
  match that.

## What to listen for when choosing a voice

Warm, wry storyteller, not a movie trailer. It should sound like a friend who knows the game's history and
is quietly enjoying the twist. A mid-pitched voice with a relaxed pace (about 150 words a minute) suits the
1930s paper look. Search the Voice Library for "documentary", "storyteller" or "narration". Audition with
this passage, which has a number, a twist and a punchline:

## 01 — Cold open: the hook, then the patent

*Audio file:* `01_cold_open.mp3`  ·  *99 words, 31.6 seconds as recorded*

*On screen:* "FIVE DECISIONS"; three $100 notes drop and "$300" is typed; four pawns, yours at $1,800 and the others at $1,500; a counter runs to 13,400,000 games. Then the 1904 patent on the desk: a slow push to "PATENTED JAN. 5, 1904", pull back to the whole board, "You know it as Monopoly."

Five decisions in Monopoly are worth three hundred dollars, every single game. Get them right, and it's like starting with three hundred dollars more than everyone else at the table. We played more than thirteen million games to find them. And the game itself? It started as a warning. In nineteen-oh-four, a woman named Lizzie Magie was granted a patent for a board game. She called it The Landlord's Game. She designed it to show how landlords get rich while everyone else pays the rent. Thirty years later, the game had a new name. You know it as Monopoly.

## 02 — The promise

*Audio file:* `02_the_promise.mp3`  ·  *about 100 words, 39 seconds*

*On screen:* Title card (frame 01). The five decisions appear as a typed list. Stamp at the end: 3 WINS FOR EVERY 2.

Most Monopoly strategy videos tell you the same thing. Buy the orange properties, because people land on them more. And sure. But you don't get to choose where you land. The dice do that. What you do choose is everything else. Whether to buy. When to trade. Whether to chase the railroads. When to build, and how much cash to keep. Whether to stay in jail. So we tested every one of those decisions. Same dice. Same board. And if you make them all well, you'll win about three games for every two that anyone else at your table wins.

## 03 — How we tested it

*Audio file:* `03_how_we_tested_it.mp3`  ·  *about 175 words, 68 seconds*

*On screen:* The board (frame 02) inks itself; witnesses sign in the Typical Player; a "locked" board with telegrams flying for the endgame deals; ticker tape counts games.

Here's how we tested it. We built a computer version of Monopoly that plays by the official rules. The real board, the real cards, auctions, mortgages, and the bank's thirty-two houses and twelve hotels. Then we taught it to play the way people actually play. Everyone buys what they land on, if they can afford it. And nobody hands you the card that finishes your set, unless you hand them the card that finishes theirs. If you've played, you know what happens next. Sometimes the board locks up. Nobody can make a fair swap, and the game just... drags. So when that happens, our players do what real families do. They start dealing. Someone sells the card you need, for a price. To test a decision, one player makes it, and the other three play like a typical player. At a four-player table, a typical player wins about twenty-five games in a hundred. A fair share. Every result you're about to see comes from a hundred thousand games. More than thirteen million games in all.

## 04 — Decision 1: Buy or pass

*Audio file:* `04_buy_or_pass.mp3`  ·  *about 237 words, 92 seconds*

*On screen:* "Fig. 2". Deed of sale stamped SOLD / PASSED; ledger ladder of cushions; auction broadside "AUCTION! KENTUCKY AVENUE"; bid ladder half / list / 1.5x.

Decision one. You land on a property nobody owns. Buy it, or pass? The careful instinct is to protect your cash, so we tried that. A player who passes whenever buying would leave them with less than five hundred dollars wins about twenty-three games in a hundred, instead of twenty-five. A small cushion is fine. Keep a hundred or two in hand and you lose nothing. But the more careful you get, the more it costs. And there's one card you never pass on: the one that finishes a set, or stops someone else from finishing theirs. If you have to mortgage something to buy it, do it. Why does buying matter so much? Because every property is a trading chip. You can't make a deal with cards you don't have. Now, in the official rules, a property you pass on doesn't just sit there. It goes to auction. So here's the other half of the decision. When someone else passes, how high do you bid? Stop at the printed price, and you're a typical player. Bid up to one and a half times the price, and you win about twenty-six and a half games in a hundred. Lowball at half price, and you drop to about twenty-four. And if your family skips the auctions, passing costs you a little less, because the property just sits there unsold, instead of going to somebody else at a bargain.

## 05 — Decision 2: Trading

*Audio file:* `05_trading.mp3`  ·  *about 213 words, 82 seconds*

*On screen:* Telegrams. "Never trades: 5 in 100" stamped REFUSED. Haggle telegram crossed out. Endgame: "WILL BUY YOUR KENTUCKY AVE" answered "DEAL STOP".

Decision two. Trading. This is the one that surprised us most. Not that trading helps. Everyone knows trading helps. It's how much it matters. A player who refuses every trade wins about five games in a hundred. Five. A fifth of a fair share. Nothing else in this video comes close. So when the deal is on the table, a set for a set, take it. And take it at the printed prices. Because the other way to ruin a trade is to haggle. "Your set is better than mine, so throw in some cash." A player who does that wins about twenty-one games in a hundred, instead of twenty-five. The other player walks away, and neither of you gets a set. What about waiting until you've got the cash to build straight away? It makes no difference. Holding out for the better set? It barely helps. And remember the endgame, when the board locks up? Someone offers to buy the card that would finish their set. Every instinct you have says no. Don't give them the set. Say yes. Players who refuse are more likely to be the first one to go broke. The cash lets you build on what you've already got. In a stalled game, the deal is the move.

## 06 — Decision 3: Railroads

*Audio file:* `06_railroads.mp3`  ·  *about 171 words, 66 seconds*

*On screen:* The four railway tickets (frame 04); the fourth arrives by telegram; stamp ONLY IF YOU TRADE.

Decision three. Every table has one. The railroad player. Buy all four, and collect two hundred dollars every time someone lands on one. Does it work? We tried three versions. A player who just buys railroads when they land on them: no better than typical. A player who chases them, mortgaging other property and bidding double at auction: still no better. Here's why. Buying alone almost never gets you all four. It happens in about four games out of a hundred. The version that works is the one that trades. When someone is holding your last railroad, offer them the card that finishes one of their colour sets. They get a set. You get your four railroads. Do that, and you'll own all four in more than a quarter of your games, you'll win about twenty-seven games in a hundred, and when you do hold all four, you win more than half of those games. So, go for the railroads only if you're willing to trade for them. Otherwise, don't bother.

## 07a — Decision 4a: Spend or save

*Audio file:* `07a_spend_or_save.mp3`  ·  *about 191 words, 74 seconds*

*On screen:* The ledger (frame 03): cushions typed line by line; second column "first to go broke" fills in red; stamp SPEND IT.

Decision four is the big one. Building. And the first question is the one every player asks. Should I spend all my cash on houses, or keep some back, in case I land on someone's hotel? Keeping a cushion feels responsible. So we gave players different cushions, and watched. Keep nothing back, and you win about twenty-five games in a hundred. Keep a hundred dollars back, about twenty-two. Three hundred, about sixteen. Five hundred, about eleven. A thousand dollars, three. But that's winning. Surely the cushion keeps you safe? It doesn't. The more cash you keep back, the more likely you are to be the first one out. We even tried the smartest-sounding version: always keep enough to pay the biggest rent on the board. That player wins about thirteen games in a hundred. Here's why. Houses are what bring the money in. Cash in your hand earns nothing. And if you do get hit, you can always mortgage, or sell houses back to the bank. So spend it. Better still, mortgage the properties you aren't building on, and spend that too. That alone takes you from twenty-five wins to twenty-eight.

## 07b — Decision 4b: How far to build

*Audio file:* `07b_how_far_to_build.mp3`  ·  *about 58 words, 22 seconds*

*On screen:* Architect's elevation drawings: one house, two, three, four, hotel; the win figure under each.

How far should you build? One house on each property almost never wins. Two houses, about four games in a hundred. Three houses, about twenty. Four, twenty-three. Hotels, twenty-five. You've probably heard that the third house is the sweet spot. It's usually the biggest jump in rent, and that's true. But stopping there costs you. Go to hotels.

## 07c — Decision 4c: Which set first

*Audio file:* `07c_which_set_first.mp3`  ·  *about 144 words, 56 seconds*

*On screen:* Sanborn map of the real blocks; two sets washed in colour; the which-first matrix as a ledger grid; orange vs green 84 / 63.

Now the question people really argue about. You own two full sets. Which one gets the houses first? We set up that exact moment. Middle of the game, every property already bought, a thousand dollars each, and you holding two sets. Then we tried every pair of sets on the board, all twenty-eight of them. The rule is simple. Build the set with the cheaper houses first. Light blue and orange beat everything. Then pink. Red and yellow are a coin flip, and so are dark blue and green. The odd one out is brown. Its rents are so low that it only goes first ahead of dark blue or green, and only just. And getting it wrong is expensive. Holding orange and green? Build orange first, and you win eighty-four games in a hundred. Build green first, sixty-three. Same two sets. Same dice.

## 07d — Decision 4d: One set or spread

*Audio file:* `07d_one_set_or_spread.mp3`  ·  *about 81 words, 31 seconds*

*On screen:* Houses spread one per lot fade out; one set builds up to hotels; "0 of 28" stamped.

So do you put hotels on one set, or a house on everything? Never a house on everything. In all twenty-eight pairs, spreading your houses evenly was never the best choice. On average, it did worse than building the wrong set first. Build one set up first. If it's a cheap set, like light blue, go straight to hotels. If it's an expensive one, like red, yellow or dark blue, get three houses on both sets first, then add the hotels.

## 07e — Decision 4e: Owe money

*Audio file:* `07e_owe_money.mp3`  ·  *about 52 words, 20 seconds*

*On screen:* A rent bill; mortgage stamps go on loose deeds first; houses stay standing.

One last building question. You land on a hotel, and you can't pay. Do you sell houses, or mortgage? Mortgage first. Sell houses last. Sell first, and you drop from about twenty-five wins to nineteen, and you're more likely to go broke first. Those houses are the only thing paying you back.

## 08 — Decision 5: Jail

*Audio file:* `08_jail.mp3`  ·  *about 125 words, 48 seconds*

*On screen:* Magie's own jail corner from the patent; ink prison bars; stamp LEAVE, then STAY once houses appear.

Decision five. You're in jail. Pay fifty dollars and get out, or stay and roll for doubles? Jail was there from the very beginning. Lizzie Magie's nineteen-oh-four board has a corner that reads: No Trespassing. Go to Jail. Here's the rule. Early in the game, get out. There's property to buy, and you want to be on the board. Once anyone has houses, stay in. You still collect your rent in jail, and every turn inside is a turn you can't land on their hotels. That's worth about one and a half games in a hundred, and it makes you less likely to be the first one out. Always staying, or always rolling once before you pay, gains you nothing. It's the timing that matters.

## 09 — Aside: the computer's player

*Audio file:* `09_the_computers_player.mp3`  ·  *about 66 words, 26 seconds*

*On screen:* A patent-style family tree: 24 boxes, winners circled, 25 generations, ending on light blue and orange washes.

One more test. We let the computer invent its own player, by trial and error. Start with twenty-four random players, keep the winners, mix and tweak their habits, and repeat, twenty-five times. It ended up with the same habits you've just heard. Plus one. It picked two favourite colours, light blue and orange, and went hard for them. It wins about thirty-six games in a hundred.

## 10 — The payoff: the playbook player

*Audio file:* `10_the_payoff.mp3`  ·  *173 words, 56.8 seconds as recorded*

*On screen:* Banknotes slide in; "$300" (the extra cash a typical player needs to keep up); "Turn it around: handing the bank $250 before you've rolled", stamped PAID TO THE BANK; game-night ledger tallies; the trophy shelf, 100 years of weekly game nights.

So put it all together. One player who makes every one of these decisions well, and never picks a favourite colour. Same dice as everyone else. How much is that worth? Remember the three hundred dollars? We gave a typical player extra cash at the start, until they won as often as the playbook player. It took three hundred dollars. Turn that around, and playing the typical way is like handing the bank two hundred and fifty dollars before you've rolled. Even without auctions, it's about two hundred dollars either way, the same as passing Go. Play these decisions well, and you win about thirty-three games in a hundred, while each of the people you're playing wins about twenty-two. That's three games for every two of theirs. And it compounds. Play once a week for a year, with the same family. The player with the playbook finishes the year as champion two years out of three. Each of the others? About one year in eleven. Same dice. No favourite colour. Just better decisions.

## 11 — The playbook

*Audio file:* `11_the_playbook.mp3`  ·  *about 126 words, 49 seconds*

*On screen:* The certificate (frame 05), rules appearing one by one; final line over the 1904 patent; end card.

Here's the playbook. One. Land on it? Buy it, unless it would leave you nearly broke. Two. At auction, bid up to one and a half times the price. Three. Make every fair set-for-set trade, and don't haggle. When the game stalls, deal. Four. Want the railroads? Trade for the last one, or don't bother. Five. Spend your cash on houses, and go to hotels. Owe money? Mortgage first. Six. Two sets? Cheaper houses first, and never one house on everything. Seven. Get out of jail early. Stay in once anyone has houses. Lizzie Magie made this game to show that the landlord always wins. Now you know how to be the landlord. Which of these does your family get wrong? Tell me in the comments.
