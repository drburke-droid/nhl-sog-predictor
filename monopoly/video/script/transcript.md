## 01 — Cold open: the hook, then the patent

*Audio file:* `01_cold_open.mp3`  ·  *107 words, 34.6 seconds as recorded*

*On screen:* "FIVE DECISIONS"; three $100 notes and a $50, and "$350"; four pawns, yours at $1,850 and theirs at $1,500; a counter to 13,400,000 games. Then the 1904 patent on the desk, with slips for the patent number and the game's name. At "Thirty years later": the Atlantic City boardwalk, and "You know it as Monopoly."

Five decisions in Monopoly are worth more than three hundred and fifty dollars, every single game. Get them right, and it's like starting with three hundred and fifty dollars more than everyone else at the table. I played more than thirteen million games to find them. The board game actually started as a warning to tenants everywhere. In nineteen-oh-four, a woman named Lizzie Magie was granted a patent for a board game. She called it The Landlord's Game. She designed it to show how landlords get rich while everyone else pays the rent. Thirty years later, the game had a new name. You know it as Monopoly.

## 02 — The promise

*Audio file:* `02_the_promise.mp3`  ·  *99 words, 29.6 seconds as recorded*

*On screen:* Title card; the orange squares; dice tumbling; the five decisions typed as a list, with "SAME DICE · SAME BOARD · TESTED ONE DECISION AT A TIME" in small capitals under a rule; stamp 55% MORE WINS.

Most Monopoly strategy videos tell you the same thing. Buy the orange properties, because people land on them more. And sure. But you don't get to choose where you land. The dice do that. What you do choose is everything else. Like whether to buy. When to trade. Whether to chase the railroads. When to build, and how much cash to keep. Whether to stay in jail. So I tested every one of those decisions. Same dice. Same board. And if you make them all well, you'll win about fifty-five percent more games than anyone else at your table.

## 03 — How I tested it

*Audio file:* `03_how_we_tested_it.mp3`  ·  *197 words, 63.9 seconds as recorded*

*On screen:* The board inks itself, the official rules ticked off; a 1922 mah-jong game night (photo); "How people actually play", two deeds swapping, A SET FOR A SET; the board padlocked, STALLED, a telegram offer, SOLD; four pawns and 25 boxes of 100 filling, "25%", the seat odds; ticker tape counting to 13,400,000.

Here's how I tested it. I built a computer version of Monopoly that plays by the official rules. The real board, the real cards, auctions, mortgages, and the bank's thirty-two houses and twelve hotels. Then I taught it to play the way people actually play. Everyone typically buys what they land on, if they can afford it. And nobody hands you the card that finishes your set, unless you hand them the card that finishes theirs. If you've played, you know what happens next. Sometimes the board locks up. Nobody can make a fair swap, and the game just... drags. So when that happens, my players do what real families do. They start dealing. Someone sells the card you need, for a price. To test a decision, one player makes it, and the other three play like a typical player. At a four-player table, a typical player wins about twenty-five percent of games. Going first helps: the first player wins about twenty-eight percent, the last about twenty-two, so every player I tested sat in every seat equally often. Every result you're about to see comes from a hundred thousand games. More than thirteen million games in all.

## 04 — Decision 1: Buy or pass

*Audio file:* `04_buy_or_pass.mp3`  ·  *255 words, 79.0 seconds as recorded*

*On screen:* Opens on a 1920 Stutz roadster under an Art Deco plaque, "Decision one: To buy, or to pass". A deed of sale; a ledger of cash cushions; the set-finishing card stamped BUY IT; deeds as trading chips; PASSED and an AUCTION poster; YOUR TOP BID AT AUCTION (half the printed price 24.1, printed price 25.1, 1.5 times 26.5); the no-auctions house rule.

Decision one. You land on a property nobody owns. Buy it, or pass? The careful instinct is to protect your cash, so I tried that. A player who passes whenever buying would leave them with less than five hundred dollars wins about twenty-three percent of games, instead of twenty-five. A small cushion is fine. Keep a hundred or two in hand and you lose nothing. But the more careful you get, the more it costs. And there's one card you never pass on: the one that finishes a set, or prevents someone else from finishing theirs. If you have to mortgage something to buy it, do it. Why does buying matter so much? Because every property is a trading chip. You can't make a deal with cards you don't have. Now, in the official rules, a property you pass on doesn't just sit there. It goes to auction. So here's the other half of the decision. When someone else passes, how high do you bid? So set your top bid before the bidding starts. Hunt for bargains, never going past half the printed price, and you drop to about twenty-four percent. Stop at the printed price, and you're a typical player. Be willing to go up to one and a half times the printed price, and you win about twenty-six and a half percent of games. And if your family skips the auctions, passing costs you a little less, because the property just sits there unsold, instead of going to somebody else at a bargain.

## 05 — Decision 2: Trading

*Audio file:* `05_trading.mp3`  ·  *368 words, 119.1 seconds as recorded*

*On screen:* Opens on the Wall Street curb market, plaque "Decision two: To trade". A swap telegram (St. James and $80 for Ventnor) stamped REFUSED, 5 of 100 boxes; the same offer ACCEPTED, with Ventnor $260 - St. James $180 = $80; the haggler's "$200" telegram crossed out ("$200, when the fair difference is $80"), NO DEAL; a ledger of other ways to trade with the light blue / orange example; the endgame offer, NO! struck through, SOLD, "if you already have a set of your own", stalled-game ledger (26.6 / 25.1 / 24.2 / 23.2), stamp SELL, IF YOU HAVE A SET.

Decision two. Trading. This is the one that surprised me most. Not that trading helps. Everyone knows trading helps. It's how much it matters. A player who refuses every trade wins about five percent of games. Five. A fifth of a fair share. Almost nothing else in this video comes close. So when the deal is on the table, a set for a set, take it. And if one side's card costs more, settle the difference at the printed prices, and nothing more. Say you swap your St. James Place, printed at one hundred and eighty dollars, for their Ventnor Avenue, at two hundred and sixty. You add the eighty-dollar difference, and that's it. Because the other way to ruin a trade is to haggle. "Your set is better than mine, so throw in two hundred." A player who does that wins about twenty-one percent, instead of twenty-five. The other player walks away, and neither of you gets a set. What about waiting to trade until you've got the cash to start building houses? The thinking is that a set does you little good until you can build on it, so why rush? It makes no difference. What about holding out for the better set? Say you hold two light blues and an orange, and they hold two oranges and a light blue. The fair swap hands them orange, the stronger set. Refusing until you get a swap where you come out with the stronger set? It barely helps. And remember the endgame, when everything is bought and there's no fair swap left? Someone might offer to buy the card that would finish their set, for two and a half times its printed price. Every instinct you have says no. Don't give them the set. But what you actually should do is say yes, as long as you already have a set of your own. The cash lets you build on what you've already got. Players who sell like that win about twenty-six and a half percent. With no set of your own, say no. And don't sell cheap: below twice the printed price, you're better off refusing. In a stalled game, the right deal is the move.

## 06 — Decision 3: Railroads

*Audio file:* `06_railroads.mp3`  ·  *293 words, 89.6 seconds as recorded*

*On screen:* Opens on Pennsylvania Station, plaque "Decision three: The railroads". Four railway tickets; a ledger of railroad players (games won, and games with all four per 100); the trade telegram as the fourth ticket arrives; the ledger again with "wins 53% of the games where it holds all four", stamp ONLY IF YOU TRADE; BUYING RAILROADS FOR CASH ($300 45.3, $400 31.3, $500 20.1, $1,000 6.0; break-even about $450).

Decision three. Every table has one. The railroad player. Buy all four, and collect two hundred dollars every time someone lands on one. Does it work? I tried three versions. A player who just buys railroads when they land on them: no better than typical. They're at the mercy of the dice, and someone else usually lands on one first and buys it. A player who chases them, mortgaging other property and bidding double at auction: still no better. Here's why. A railroad only goes to auction when someone passes on it, and almost everyone buys a railroad when they land on it. So either way, buying them outright or at auction gets you all four in only about four games out of a hundred. In the rest, someone else buys at least one before you get the chance. The version that works is the one that trades. When someone is holding your last railroad, offer them the card that finishes one of their colour sets. They get a set. You get your four railroads. Trade like that, and you end up holding all four in about twenty-eight games out of a hundred, instead of four. In those games, you win more than half the time. Across all your games, that lifts your win rate from twenty-five percent to twenty-seven. So go for the railroads only if you're willing to trade for them, and trade for them as well as your colour sets, not instead of them. And if someone will sell you railroads for cash? Paying up to about four hundred dollars each still pays off. Pay five hundred or more, and you'd win more often by not bothering. At a thousand dollars each, you'd win only about six percent of games.

## 07a — Decision 4a: Spend or save

*Audio file:* `07a_spend_or_save.mp3`  ·  *185 words, 66.7 seconds as recorded*

*On screen:* Opens on the Hotel Traymore, Atlantic City, 1920, plaque "Decision four: To build". The cushion ledger (wins, then first to go broke in red); "Always keep enough to pay the biggest rent", 13%; houses earn, cash doesn't; stamp SPEND IT; mortgage to build, 25% to 28%.

Decision four is the big one. Building. And the first question is the one every player asks. Should I spend all my cash on houses, or keep some back, in case I land on someone's hotel? Keeping a cushion feels responsible. So I gave players different cushions, and watched. Keep nothing back, and you win about twenty-five percent. Keep a hundred dollars back, about twenty-two. Three hundred, about sixteen. Five hundred, about eleven. A thousand dollars, three. But that's winning. Surely the cushion keeps you safe? It doesn't. The more cash you keep back, the more likely you are to be the first one out. I even tried the smartest-sounding version: always keep enough to pay the biggest rent on the board. That player wins about thirteen percent. Here's why. Houses are what bring the money in. Cash in your hand earns nothing. And if you do get hit, you can always mortgage, or sell houses back to the bank. So spend it. Better still, mortgage the properties you aren't building on, and spend that too. That alone takes you from twenty-five percent to twenty-eight.

## 07b — Decision 4b: How far to build

*Audio file:* `07b_how_far_to_build.mp3`  ·  *55 words, 22.0 seconds as recorded*

*On screen:* Five bars rising, one house to hotels, each topped with its drawing; the third house ringed, "the sweet spot?"; stamp GO TO HOTELS.

How far should you build? One house on each property almost never wins. Two houses, about four percent. Three houses, about twenty. Four, twenty-three. Hotels, twenty-five. You've probably heard that the third house is the sweet spot. It's usually the biggest jump in rent, and that's true. But stopping there costs you. Go to hotels.

## 07c — Decision 4c: Which set first

*Audio file:* `07c_which_set_first.mp3`  ·  *144 words, 48.5 seconds as recorded*

*On screen:* Orange and green deeds, "Which one first?"; a grid ticking off all 28 pairs; THE RULE with the sets ranked by house cost; orange first 84% vs green first 63%; "Same two sets. Same dice."

Now the question people really argue about. You own two full sets. Which one gets the houses first? I set up that exact moment. Middle of the game, every property already bought, a thousand dollars each, and you holding two sets. Then I tried every pair of sets on the board, all twenty-eight of them. The rule is simple. Build the set with the cheaper houses first. Light blue and orange beat everything. Then pink. Red and yellow are a coin flip, and so are dark blue and green. The odd one out is brown. Its rents are so low that it only goes first ahead of dark blue or green, and only just. And getting it wrong is expensive. Holding orange and green? Build orange first, and you win eighty-four percent of the time. Build green first, sixty-three. Same two sets. Same dice.

## 07d — Decision 4d: One set or spread

*Audio file:* `07d_one_set_or_spread.mp3`  ·  *81 words, 25.5 seconds as recorded*

*On screen:* Hotels on one set vs a house on everything; the spread crossed out, BEST IN 0 OF 28; average wins 67.6% vs 65.8%; cards for a cheap set (straight to hotels) and an expensive set (three houses on both, then hotels).

So do you put hotels on one set, or a house on everything? Never a house on everything. In all twenty-eight pairs, spreading your houses evenly was never the best choice. On average, it did worse than building the wrong set first. Build one set up first. If it's a cheap set, like light blue, go straight to hotels. If it's an expensive one, like red, yellow or dark blue, get three houses on both sets first, then add the hotels.

## 07e — Decision 4e: Owe money

*Audio file:* `07e_owe_money.mp3`  ·  *63 words, 23.0 seconds as recorded*

*On screen:* A $1,100 rent bill, CAN'T PAY; "Sell houses? Or mortgage?"; loose deeds stamped MORTGAGED while the houses stay standing; sell first 19.1%; stamp MORTGAGE FIRST.

One last building question. You land on a hotel, and you can't pay. Do you sell houses, or mortgage? Mortgage first: everything without houses on it, like loose properties, railroads and utilities. Sell houses last. Sell first, and you drop from about twenty-five percent to nineteen, and you're more likely to go broke first. Those houses are the only thing paying you back.

## 08 — Decision 5: Jail

*Audio file:* `08_jail.mp3`  ·  *125 words, 39.7 seconds as recorded*

*On screen:* Opens on a 1921 Prohibition raid in New York, plaque "Decision five: Jail". A pawn behind inked bars, PAY $50 vs STAY; Magie's own jail corner on the 1904 patent; THE RULE, early GET OUT vs STAY IN once there are houses; a jail ledger, stamp TIMING.

Decision five. You're in jail. Pay fifty dollars and get out, or stay and roll for doubles? Jail was there from the very beginning. Lizzie Magie's nineteen-oh-four board has a corner that reads: No Trespassing. Go to Jail. Here's the rule. Early in the game, get out. There's property to buy, and you want to be on the board. Once anyone has houses, stay in. You still collect your rent in jail, and every turn inside is a turn you can't land on their hotels. That takes you from twenty-five percent to twenty-six and a half, and it makes you less likely to be the first one out. Always staying, or always rolling once before you pay, gains you nothing. It's the timing that matters.

## 11 — The playbook

*Audio file:* `11_the_playbook.mp3`  ·  *135 words, 49.8 seconds as recorded*

*On screen:* The certificate, THE PLAYBOOK, rules I to VII typed as they're read (III: "Stalled? Sell only with a set."), the wax seal "+55% MORE WINS"; a fade to the 1904 patent; "Now you know how to be the landlord."; "Which of these does your family get wrong?"; the Hasbro disclaimer.

Here's the playbook. One. Land on it? Buy it, unless it would leave you nearly broke. Two. At auction, bid up to one and a half times the price. Three. Make every fair set-for-set trade, and don't haggle. When the game stalls, sell only if you have a set of your own. Four. Want the railroads? Trade for the last one, or don't bother. Five. Spend your cash on houses, and go to hotels. Owe money? Mortgage first. Six. Two sets? Cheaper houses first, and never one house on everything. Seven. Get out of jail early. Stay in once anyone has houses. Lizzie Magie made this game to show that the landlord always wins. Now you know how to be the landlord. Which of these does your family get wrong?
