# Art direction: "the patent drawing comes to life"

Companion to `Video plan - decisions you control.md`. Style frames (rendered from code, so every element can
be animated the same way): `art/frames/01_title.png` to `06_thumbnail.png`, made by `art/style_frames.py`.

## The idea

We can't use Hasbro's board, logo, characters, cards, money or tokens. We don't need them. Monopoly's real
history left a paper trail that is richer than the box art and mostly public domain:

1. **The patent, 1904.** Lizzie Magie patented *The Landlord's Game* (US Patent 748,626, granted 5 January
   1904) to show how landlords get rich at everyone else's expense. Her drawing already has the four corners,
   railroads, a jail ("NO TRESPASSING — GO TO JAIL") and a start square ("LABOR UPON MOTHER EARTH PRODUCES
   WAGES"). It's a US patent drawing, so it's public domain, and it looks wonderful.
2. **Atlantic City, 1900s-1930s.** The squares on the board we play today are real streets: Boardwalk,
   Illinois, Kentucky, Ventnor, Marvin Gardens, the railroads. There are public-domain photographs, postcards
   and fire-insurance maps of every one of them.
3. **The money, 1935.** The game became a hit in the Depression. Ledgers, banknote engraving, rubber stamps,
   telegrams, railway tickets and stock certificates are the period's own paperwork, and they're perfect
   containers for our numbers.

So the whole video looks as if it had been assembled from that paper trail. The board is our own drawing in
the manner of Magie's patent. Results are typed into ledgers and stamped. Trades arrive as telegrams.
Railroads are tickets, and the playbook is a certificate. Nothing on screen is Hasbro's, and every image has a
reason to be there.

Why it helps the video, not just the look:
- **The lore is a hook.** Most viewers don't know the game started as a 1904 patent by a woman warning about
  landlords, or that the squares are real Atlantic City streets. That's a 20-second cold open nobody else has
  used for a strategy video.
- **Every number gets a physical object.** A win rate is a ledger line, a verdict is a rubber stamp, a trade
  is a telegram. Abstract statistics become things the viewer can picture.
- **It stands apart.** Every other Monopoly video shows the same Hasbro board. This one is recognisably
  Monopoly but looks like nothing else on the platform.

## Three visual sources and how each is used

| Source | What we take | Where it appears |
|---|---|---|
| **The 1904 patent** | The drawing itself (cold open, history beat); its draftsman lettering, hatching and "Fig. 1" numbering; its corner phrases | Cold open; the board (frame 02) is drawn in this style; each decision is a "Fig." (Fig. 2, To buy or to pass...); "Witnesses: the Typical Player, the Playbook Player" introduces the two players; the jail section uses Magie's own "NO TRESPASSING — GO TO JAIL" |
| **Atlantic City** | Period photos of the real streets; linen postcards; Sanborn fire-insurance maps (their pink and yellow building colours sit naturally beside the board's colour groups) | A two-second "this is the real Illinois Avenue" postcard whenever a property is named; Sanborn map pans as transitions between decisions; the two-set section plays on a map of the real blocks |
| **1935 money** | Ledger paper, banknote guilloche borders, rubber stamps, telegrams, railway tickets, certificates, ticker tape | Results tables (ledger, frame 03); verdicts (stamps); trades and endgame deals (telegrams, frame 04); railroads (tickets, frame 04); the playbook (certificate, frame 05); the game counter (ticker tape) |

## Palette

| Role | Colour | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Paper | `#EFE4CC` | Every background, with grain, mottling and a soft vignette |
| Ink | `#1F1A14` | Lines, type, hatching |
| Sepia | `#6E4E2E` | Secondary type, annotations |
| Stamp red | `#A3271F` | Verdicts, warnings, the one number that matters on a page |
| Banknote green | `#2E5B4A` | Guilloche borders, section headings, ledger rules |
| Brass | `#A8833A` | Pawns and highlights |

The eight colour groups appear only as **watercolour washes** in muted printer's inks (brown `#8A5A3C`, light
blue `#82B2CC`, pink `#C46892`, orange `#D98A3D`, red `#B8473A`, yellow `#D6B546`, green `#4E8A5B`, dark blue
`#3C5A8A`). They're the same hues players know, but hand-tinted rather than Hasbro's flat print colours. When
the video talks about one colour group, that group's washes deepen and the others fade.

## How things move

- **Ink draws itself.** Board lines are stroked on with a slight wobble, as if inked by a draftsman.
  Watercolour washes bleed into the colour bands.
- **Numbers are typed.** Ledger figures appear one keystroke at a time, with a typewriter sound.
- **Verdicts are stamped.** The stamp drops in over three frames with a small camera shake and a thud. Ink
  coverage is deliberately uneven (already in the frames).
- **Trades are telegrams.** Strips type out word by word ("WILL GIVE YOU KENTUCKY AVE STOP"), with a
  telegraph-key sound.
- **The pawn is a brass pawn** (our design, not a Hasbro token). It hops square by square with a wooden tick.
- **Camera:** slow push-ins on paper, gentle parallax between layers (paper, ink, objects with drop shadows),
  page turns or a slide across the desk between decisions. Add light film grain and a slight gate weave
  throughout.
- **The 13.4 million games** run as a ticker tape counting up in the corner during "how we tested it".

## Sound

- **Effects:** typewriter, rubber stamp, telegraph key, cash-register bell (for "you won"), dice on a wooden
  table, a pen nib scratching as lines draw on.
- **Music:** ragtime piano or 1930s swing, whichever fits the narration.
  - Scott Joplin's compositions are public domain, but a recording has its own copyright. Use a
    public-domain recording, or a royalty-free modern performance (for example from YouTube's Audio Library
    or Musopen).
  - US sound recordings published before 1926 are public domain as of 2026 under the Music Modernization
    Act; check the exact recording before using it.

## Thumbnail

Frame 06: a close crop of our board's corner (Boardwalk, Park Place, the "WAGES" corner) with the brass pawn;
"SAVE IT" crossed out in red; a big "SPEND IT" stamp; "33 wins, not 25". Alternatives using the same parts:
- the patent drawing with a red stamp across it reading "WHAT YOU CONTROL";
- the four railway tickets with "ONLY IF YOU TRADE".

## Keeping clear of Hasbro

| Avoid | Use instead |
|---|---|
| The MONOPOLY logo and wordmark styling | The word "Monopoly" in plain type in the title and narration only; "Not affiliated with or endorsed by Hasbro" in the description and end card |
| Mr. Monopoly (Rich Uncle Pennybags), the jail officer, any Hasbro character | No mascot. The two "players" are our brass pawns and signatures |
| Hasbro's board design (corner illustrations, GO arrow, red-banner styling) | Our board drawn in the 1904 patent style (frame 02) |
| Chance "?" and Community Chest treasure-chest art; Hasbro card layouts | Plain lettered squares; any card shown is a period document of our own design |
| Title Deed card layout | Period deeds of sale, bills of sale, railway tickets |
| Monopoly money | Engraved banknote borders of our own (guilloche, as in frames 01 and 05) |
| Metal tokens (top hat, car, dog, boot, ship...) | Brass pawns (our design) |
| Green houses and red hotels | Architect's elevation drawings, or plain unpainted wooden blocks |

The rules, the square names (real streets) and the board's layout of squares are facts, not artwork. I'm not
a lawyer: if the channel earns money, get a quick check of the title and thumbnail wording before publishing.

## Lore lines safe to use on camera

From the research notes (`research_notes/.../expert_strategy_rules_history.md`):
- Magie's patent, 748,626, was granted on 5 January 1904. The game came with two sets of rules, an
  "anti-monopolist" set and a "monopolist" set. In 1902 she called it "a practical demonstration of the present
  system of land-grabbing".
- Parker Brothers turned Charles Darrow down in October 1934 ("too complicated, too technical, [and it] took
  too long to play") and bought the game on 18 March 1935. Magie reportedly received $500.
- About 275 million copies sold.

Flag as "reportedly", or check before use: the "52 fundamental errors" story is a myth (don't use it); claims
about real money in WWII escape-kit sets are disputed.
